# How to Give Feedback Across US and Italian Communication Styles

> US feedback is direct and explicit. Italian feedback is often contextual. Here is how to bridge both styles with your Italian team without losing clarity.



You hired someone in Italy. You gave clear feedback. Nothing changed. Or the reverse: your Italian manager gave you a careful, gentle update, and you missed that it was a warning.

This is rarely about skill or effort. It is about two different ways of saying hard things. Once you see the difference, you can work with both.

## Two styles, two assumptions

US feedback is usually direct. You say what worked, what did not, and what to change. The words carry the message. A good manager is clear, and clarity is seen as respect.

Italian feedback often relies on context. The message sits in the tone, the timing, the setting and the relationship. A direct criticism, especially in front of others, can feel harsh or even personal. A good manager protects the relationship first and then raises the issue, often indirectly.

Neither style is better. Each one assumes the listener will fill in the gaps in a certain way. Problems start when the listener fills them in the other way.

## What goes wrong

A US founder says, "This report needs work." In the US, that is a mild note. An Italian employee may hear it as a sharp judgment and go quiet.

An Italian team lead says, "Maybe we could look again at this section." To them, that is a clear request. A US founder may read it as a suggestion and move on.

Both sides acted in good faith. Both sides misread the other.

## Rules for US managers giving feedback in Italy

**Do it in private.** Public correction damages trust fast. Use a one-to-one call or meeting.

**Open with the relationship.** Take a moment to ask how things are going. This is not wasted time. It sets the frame in which your feedback will be heard.

**Be specific, but frame it as shared work.** Say "Let's look at how we can make this section stronger" instead of "This section is wrong." Then name the exact change you need.

**Do not hide the point.** Softening is fine. Vagueness is not. If you need something fixed, say what, and say by when. Your Italian colleague will respect a clear request delivered with care.

**Pair criticism with real recognition.** Mention what the person does well, and mean it. Skip the formula of praise, criticism, praise. It reads as scripted.

**Check understanding.** Ask, "What would you change first?" Their answer shows you whether the message landed.

## Rules for reading Italian feedback as a US manager

When an Italian colleague raises something, treat soft wording as a real signal.

- "It might be complicated" often means "this will not work as planned."
- "We could consider" often means "I think we should."
- A long pause or a change of subject can mean disagreement.

When you sense hesitation, ask a follow-up in private. Try, "What concerns do you have?" or "What would make this easier?" Give them room to be honest without losing face.

## Set the rules together

The most useful step is to talk about the gap itself. In a new team, say it openly:

"I tend to be very direct. It is not meant as criticism of you. If something I say sounds harsh, tell me. And please tell me directly when you see a problem."

This does two things. It gives your Italian colleagues permission to be more explicit with you. It also signals that your bluntness is a style, not a verdict.

Agree on a few habits:

- A regular one-to-one where feedback goes both ways.
- A shared way to flag urgency, such as a word or a label, so a soft tone never hides a real deadline.
- A written follow-up after any serious conversation, so the agreed actions are clear to everyone.

## Written feedback needs extra care

Email and chat remove tone. A short, direct message that feels efficient in the US can feel cold in Italy. Add a greeting. Add a sentence of context. Keep the request itself plain and easy to find.

For anything sensitive, speak first and write after. A call lets you adjust as you go. The written note then confirms what you both agreed.

## Performance problems

If the issue is serious or repeated, do not rely on hints. Be explicit and calm. Describe the facts, the effect on the work, and what must change. Keep a record of what was said. Italian employment rules shape how formal steps must be handled, so involve your local HR or employment partner before any formal action.

## The goal

You are not trying to become Italian, and your team is not trying to become American. You are building a shared style: clear enough for the US side, respectful enough for the Italian side. Teams that do this well move faster, because people say what they mean and hear what is meant.
