# The Non-Taxable Wage Component for Lower Earners in Italy: How It Works in Payroll

> How Italy's tax-wedge relief for lower earners works in payroll: a non-taxable share of income at low bands, plus an extra deduction in the middle bands.



Italy has a relief for lower earners that works inside payroll. It is often called the tax-wedge relief. It has two parts. One is a non-taxable share of employment income. The other is an extra tax deduction for the next income band. This article explains how each part works and what it means for your payroll process.

## What the tax wedge is

The tax wedge is the gap between what an employer pays and what the employee takes home. Taxes and contributions sit in that gap. The relief described here narrows it for people on lower pay. The employer does not claim anything. The relief is applied in the monthly payroll run.

## Part one: the non-taxable amount

For lower earners, a share of employment income is treated as non-taxable for personal income tax (IRPEF). The share depends on the income band:

| Employment income | Non-taxable share of that income |
|---|---|
| Up to EUR 8,500 | 7.1% |
| EUR 8,500 to EUR 15,000 | 5.3% |
| EUR 15,000 to EUR 20,000 | 4.8% |

The share is a percentage of employment income. It is not a flat sum. The lowest band gets the highest share. The share steps down as income rises.

For a founder, the practical point is simple. The employee's taxable base is smaller than the gross pay. Net pay goes up. Your gross cost does not change.

## Part two: the additional deduction

The second part covers the band above the first. Employees earning between EUR 20,000 and EUR 32,000 receive an additional deduction of EUR 1,000 per year. Above EUR 32,000 the deduction shrinks. It reaches zero at EUR 40,000.

This is a phase-out, not a cliff. The amount falls gradually across the range between EUR 32,000 and EUR 40,000. A raise inside that range still increases net pay, but by less than the same raise would elsewhere.

## What this means at payroll level

Payroll handles both parts, so you rarely touch them directly. Still, a few points matter for planning.

**Gross pay drives the result.** The relief depends on the level of employment income. When you set a salary, the band decides which treatment applies. Two offers that look close on paper can produce different net amounts if they fall on opposite sides of a threshold.

**Net pay is not a straight line.** In the lower bands the non-taxable share changes as income crosses a threshold. In the upper range the extra deduction fades out. Both effects mean that net pay does not rise evenly with gross pay.

**Income is measured on the year.** The thresholds refer to annual amounts. A mid-year hire or a mid-year raise needs a payroll provider that can estimate the annual figure and adjust. Otherwise the relief may be over- or under-applied during the year and corrected later.

**Offers should be shown as gross and net.** Candidates in Italy compare net pay. If you only quote gross, the relief is invisible to them. Showing both avoids confusion in the first payslip.

## What to check before you rely on this

The figures above come from a secondary source. The rules can be updated by each annual budget law, and payroll software applies the official tables. Before you build a salary offer around a threshold, confirm the current figures with your payroll provider or an Italian labor consultant.

Also confirm how the relief interacts with other items in the payslip. Those items include other deductions and any regional or municipal taxes. Each of them has its own rules. The relief described here is one layer in the calculation, not the whole result.

## A simple way to work with it

Start from the target net pay for the role. Work backward to the gross salary. Check which band that gross falls in. Then check whether a small change in gross would move the employee into a different treatment. If it would, decide whether the move is worth it for both sides.
