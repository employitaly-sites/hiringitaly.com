# How to Write a Job Post That Attracts Italian Candidates

> Write job posts that work in Italy: which language to use, how to frame salary and benefits, and which channels reach Italian candidates.



You can post the same job in New York and Milan. You will not get the same response. Italian candidates read job posts differently, look for different signals, and use different channels. A few changes to your post will help.

## Write the post in Italian, or in both languages

Many Italian professionals read English well. Most still search for jobs in Italian. If your post is English only, you lose people who filter by Italian keywords. You also signal that the role is for a remote contractor, not a real local hire.

The best approach is a full Italian version with an English version below it. Do not machine translate and publish. A stiff translation reads as careless. Have a native speaker check it.

Use the Italian job title people actually search for. Some titles stay in English, such as Product Manager or Software Engineer. Others do not translate well. Check how Italian companies title the same role and copy that.

If the working language of the team is English, say so. Name the language level you expect. "Fluent English required" is clear. "Excellent communication skills" is not.

## Be clear about the employer and the contract

Italian candidates want to know who employs them. A post from a foreign company with no Italian entity raises questions. Will they be on a proper payroll? Will they have the usual protections? Will they be paid on time?

Say it plainly. State that the role is a permanent employment contract under Italian rules, if it is. If you hire through an Employer of Record, say that the person will be employed in Italy and will work day to day with your team. This removes the main doubt.

Also state the collective agreement that applies, if you know it. Candidates in Italy expect this level of detail, and it shows you understand the market.

## Frame salary the way Italians think about it

Most Italians talk about pay as gross annual salary. The common term is RAL, which stands for Retribuzione Annuale Lorda. Use it. State a gross annual figure or range in euros.

Do not quote a monthly net figure. Do not convert from a dollar number without checking it. A candidate who sees a dollar salary will convert it and compare it with local offers. If the number looks off, they will leave.

Explain how the annual figure is paid. In Italy, many contracts spread the annual amount over more monthly payments than twelve. If your structure differs, say so. Confusion here is a common reason offers fall apart late in the process.

Include a salary range. Posts without any pay information get fewer applications. Candidates assume the figure is low or negotiable in your favor. A range also saves time for both sides.

Mention the severance accrual (TFR) only if it matters to your pitch. It is standard, so it is not a selling point. The more useful message is that the package is complete and legal.

## Show benefits that matter locally

US benefit lists do not always translate. Health insurance, for example, is less of a draw because public healthcare exists. A supplementary plan can still help, but do not lead with it.

What Italian candidates often care about:

- Flexible or hybrid work, and clear rules on it
- Meal vouchers or welfare budgets
- Training and a clear growth path
- Stable contract terms
- Working hours that respect personal time

Describe the rules, not the slogans. "Hybrid, with office days agreed by the team" is better than "flexible culture."

## Keep the tone plain

Italian professionals are wary of hype. Avoid phrases such as "rockstar," "ninja," or "fast-paced family." They read as empty. Describe the work, the team, the tools, and the goals. Say who the person reports to.

Keep requirements honest. A long list of must-haves discourages good applicants. Separate what is required from what is useful.

Tell candidates what the process looks like. List the stages. Say how long you take to reply. Italian candidates often have a notice period to serve, so say early if you need a fast start.

## Choose channels that perform

LinkedIn is the main channel for professional roles in Italy. Post there, and write the post in Italian. Ask your Italian team members or advisers to share it.

General job boards also work, especially for roles outside tech. Pick the ones Italian candidates already use, and avoid ones that only exist in your home market.

For junior and mid-level roles, university career services are worth the effort. Many schools run their own boards and events.

For technical roles, go where the community is. Local meetups, online groups, and open source circles are strong sources. A direct message from an engineer on your team often does more than any ad.

Referrals matter a lot. Italian professional networks are tight. If you have even one person in Italy, ask for introductions.

## A short checklist

- Italian text first, English second
- Local job title
- Clear employer and contract type
- Gross annual salary range in euros
- Specific benefits and work rules
- Plain tone, honest requirements
- Posted on LinkedIn plus one or two targeted channels

Get these right and you will see better applicants and fewer drop-offs at the offer stage.
