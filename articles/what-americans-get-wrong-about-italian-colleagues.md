# What Americans Get Wrong About Italian Colleagues

> Long lunches, endless meetings, no sense of urgency? A plain look at common stereotypes about Italian coworkers and what working with them is really like.



Many US founders arrive with a picture of Italian work life. It comes from movies, holiday trips and a few stories from friends. Some of it has a grain of truth. Most of it leads to bad management choices.

This article looks at the common stereotypes and compares them with what US teams usually find once they hire in Italy.

## Stereotype: Italians do not work hard

This is the most common one, and it is the least useful. Italian professionals tend to take pride in their craft. They care about doing a task properly, not just quickly. Engineers, accountants, designers and sales people on Italian teams often put in real effort and think hard about quality.

The difference is in how effort is shown. In the US, visible busyness is often read as commitment. Fast replies, packed calendars and late-night messages signal dedication. In Italy, a person may be less visible but still deliver strong work. Judge output, not noise.

## Stereotype: Long lunches mean lost time

Meals matter in Italy. Many colleagues will step away to eat properly and talk. An American manager may see this as slack. In practice, the meal is often where relationships form and problems get solved informally.

If you schedule a call over the lunch break, expect friction. If you join the table now and then, expect trust to grow faster than it would over a dozen status updates.

## Stereotype: Everything is relaxed and informal

This one surprises people. Italian workplaces can be quite formal. Titles are used. First names may not be offered right away. Emails often open with a polite greeting and close with a courteous line. Skipping this can come across as cold or careless.

The warmth is real, but it comes after respect is shown. A US manager who jumps straight to casual language may lose some goodwill without knowing it.

## Stereotype: Italians do not respect deadlines

Deadlines are real in Italy. What differs is how they are negotiated. Italian colleagues often prefer to talk through a deadline and adjust it by discussion. An American team may treat a date as fixed once it is written down. An Italian teammate may treat it as a point to revisit if circumstances change.

The fix is simple. State which deadlines are firm and why. Ask directly whether the plan is realistic. Do this early, not on the day before delivery.

## Stereotype: Meetings are chaotic

Meetings in Italy can feel looser than in US companies. People may speak at the same time. Side conversations are common. The agenda may not be followed to the letter.

This does not always mean a lack of focus. Discussion is often how people reach agreement. Decisions may be shaped before and after the meeting, in smaller conversations. If you want clear outcomes, send a written summary afterward and confirm owners for each action. Most teams welcome this.

## Stereotype: Italians are not direct

Americans often say Italians talk around the point. The reality is more mixed. Italians can be very expressive and open about opinions. They may also soften bad news in a way that sounds vague to a US ear.

If you need a clear yes or no, ask for it in plain words. Ask what could go wrong. Ask what support is needed. People usually answer honestly when the question is specific.

## Stereotype: Time off is treated as sacred, so nothing gets done

Italian workers value their time away from work, and they expect to disconnect. This is part of the culture and it is protected by the way employment is set up in the country. A US manager who expects replies during holidays or evenings will meet resistance.

This is not laziness. It is a different view of balance. Teams that respect rest often see steadier performance across the year. Plan around absences instead of fighting them.

## What to do as a US employer

A few habits help most.

- Define outcomes clearly and leave room for the person to choose how to reach them.
- Use polite, formal tone at the start, then let the relationship relax over time.
- Mark firm deadlines as firm, and explain the reason.
- Follow every meeting with a short written summary.
- Ask specific questions when you need a direct answer.
- Respect time off and plan coverage ahead.

None of this requires you to change your company. It asks you to read the signals correctly. Most conflicts between US managers and Italian staff come from misreading style as substance.

## The practical side

Culture is one part of hiring in Italy. Pay, contributions and employer obligations are another. Understanding both helps you make a fair offer and plan your budget before you commit.
