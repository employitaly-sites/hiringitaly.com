# Biweekly US Payroll vs Monthly Italian Payroll: What Changes for Cash Flow

> US teams pay biweekly. Italy pays monthly. See how the difference affects your cash flow, your forecasts and what your Italian hires expect.



If your US team is paid biweekly, you are used to a steady rhythm. Money leaves the account every other week. Employees see a deposit on a fixed Friday. Finance plans around it.

Italy works differently. Employees are normally paid monthly. That sounds like a small detail. It is not. It changes your cash flow, your accruals and what your new hire expects on pay day.

This article covers the practical differences. It does not replace advice on your specific contract or collective agreement.

## The basic difference

In the US, biweekly payroll is common. Pay periods are short. Each check is a smaller slice of annual pay. Some months contain an extra pay run, and finance teams know to plan for it.

In Italy, the standard is one payslip per month. The employee receives their net salary once, usually at or near the end of the month. The payslip covers the whole month and shows gross pay, withholdings, social contributions and net pay.

There is no extra pay run in some months. Each month looks the same on the calendar.

## What this means for your cash flow

**Larger single outflows.** A monthly run moves a bigger amount at once. Your treasury needs to hold enough cash on the payment date. If your US payroll is spread across the month, the Italian run will feel lumpy by comparison.

**Contributions and taxes follow a monthly cycle.** Employer social contributions and employee withholdings are tied to the monthly payroll. They are due on a schedule after the pay month closes. Your payroll provider or EOR should give you a calendar. Build it into your forecast so these dates do not surprise you.

**Fewer pay runs, more predictability.** One run per month is simpler to reconcile. You close the books on one payroll, not a mix of partial periods. For many finance teams this is easier once the first cycle is done.

**Accruals work differently.** With biweekly pay, some salary expense is earned but unpaid at month end. With monthly pay in arrears, the gap can be larger. Check how your accounting treats this so your month-end close stays accurate.

## Additional payments beyond the monthly salary

Many Italian employment contracts include more than the regular monthly payment. Collective agreements often provide extra salary payments at set times of the year, and certain amounts are accrued for the employee over time and settled when employment ends.

The details depend on the collective agreement that applies to the role. Do not assume your US compensation structure maps over. Ask for the full annual cost, not just the monthly figure. Cash flow is lumpy in the months when these extra payments fall due.

This is where US teams most often underestimate the real cost. A salary quoted as an annual number and divided by the pay periods you are used to will not match what you actually pay.

## What employees expect

Your Italian hires will not expect a biweekly deposit. They know monthly pay and plan their finances around it. Rent, loans and household bills line up with one monthly income.

Three expectations matter most.

**Reliability on the pay date.** A late payment is a serious problem. Employees count on the money arriving when it always does. Set the date once and keep it.

**A clear payslip.** The monthly payslip is a formal document. Employees read it. They expect to understand each line. If you work with a provider, make sure payslips are available and explained in a way your hire can follow.

**No pressure to move to biweekly.** Some US founders ask whether they can match their home schedule. It is rarely worth it. The monthly rhythm is the local norm, and your employee will not see biweekly pay as a benefit. It may only create extra admin.

## How to prepare

1. **Map the pay calendar.** Fix the monthly payment date and the dates for contributions and withholdings. Share it with finance.
2. **Forecast the full annual cost.** Include the extra payments and the amounts accrued over time. Do not forecast from base salary alone.
3. **Align your accounting.** Decide how to accrue salary at month end so reports stay consistent across the US and Italian entities.
4. **Fund the account early.** Make sure cash is in place before the pay date, since the amount is larger than a single biweekly run.
5. **Explain the schedule to your hire.** A short note on when they will be paid and what the payslip shows avoids confusion in the first month.

## Practical takeaway

Monthly pay is not worse than biweekly. It is different. Your cash moves in bigger steps, your calendar has fewer pay events, and your employee plans around one reliable date.

Treat the Italian schedule as a local standard. Build your forecast around it from the first day. Your finance team will thank you, and your new hire will start with confidence in how they are paid.
