# Building Career Paths for Italian Employees in US-Led Teams

> How US-led companies can give Italian hires real growth paths, and avoid turning them into a remote outpost with no future.



You hired well in Italy. The person is skilled, motivated and productive. Then, a year later, they leave. The exit interview says the same thing it always says: nowhere to go.

This is the remote outpost trap. A team member sits in another country, works in another time zone, and slowly drops out of the company's promotion logic. Nobody decided this. It just happens when growth paths are designed around the people in the headquarters.

Here is how to avoid it.

## Why the trap forms

US-led teams tend to promote what they see. Managers notice the person who speaks up in the hallway, joins the late call, or answers fast on chat. An employee in Italy is often offline when the decisions get made. Over time, the work is the same but the visibility is not.

There is a second cause. Many US companies have no written definition of what each level means. Promotions run on trust and proximity. That works poorly across borders, and it works worst for people who are far from the center.

The fix is not a special Italy program. The fix is to make growth rules explicit for everyone, then check that they work in Italy.

## Write down the levels

Start with a career ladder. For each role family, define the levels in plain terms. What scope does the person own? What decisions can they make alone? What impact do they have beyond their own tasks?

Keep the language about outcomes, not hours or presence. If a level requires "strong executive presence," rewrite it. Say what you actually need: clear written updates, sound judgment on tradeoffs, the ability to lead a project without close supervision.

Then share the ladder with every employee. Italian hires should see it on day one, not discover it at review time.

## Give real ownership, not leftover work

Outposts often get the stable, well-defined tasks. The new, risky and visible projects stay at headquarters. That caps growth.

Do the opposite on purpose. Assign Italian team members to projects with clear business results. Let them present the work to leadership. Let them own a product area, a customer segment or a region, such as Southern Europe.

Ownership is what makes a promotion case. If the person never owned anything, the case is empty.

## Make the work visible in writing

Time zones make live visibility hard. So build visibility into documents.

- Ask for short written project updates in a shared place.
- Record decisions and the reasons behind them.
- Keep promotion cases in writing, with examples of impact.
- Share wins in channels everyone reads, not in private chats.

Written work travels across time zones. It also helps your US-based staff, who benefit from the same habits.

## Fix the meeting schedule

If every important meeting happens at a time that suits only the US, the Italian team member will always be a guest. Rotate times where you can. Record the sessions and share notes. Move decisions out of live meetings and into written proposals with a comment window.

Also plan for the cost of being late in the day. Do not make the same person take the late call every week. Share the burden or protect their evenings.

## Connect them to a manager who can advocate

A manager's job in a promotion cycle is to argue for their people. A distant manager who rarely interacts with the report will argue weakly.

Hold regular one-on-ones that are about growth, not only status. Ask what the person wants in the next stage of their career. Set goals together. Make sure the manager attends calibration or promotion discussions with evidence in hand.

If the manager has several remote reports and a large local team, check that the remote ones are not at the bottom of the attention list.

## Offer mobility, not only stasis

Some Italian employees will want to move into leadership. Others will want to become deep specialists. Some may want a stint at headquarters or in another office. Offer all of these paths where the business allows.

A lateral move or a short assignment abroad can signal that the company sees the person as part of the whole. If you plan relocation or visas, handle them early with your Employer of Record (EOR) or advisors, so the person is not left waiting.

## Review pay and titles on the same terms

An Italian employee should be evaluated against the same ladder as a US colleague. Titles should mean the same thing. Pay will differ by local market, but the logic for raises and promotions should not. If the process is different, people will notice, and trust will go.

## Check the results

Track simple signals. Are Italian employees promoted at a similar rate to peers? Do they hold visible projects? Do they stay? Ask in surveys whether they see a future at the company.

If the answers are weak, change the process before you lose the next person.

## The short version

An outpost is a team without a path. Remove the causes: unclear levels, hidden work, distant managers and meeting schedules built for one time zone. Give real ownership, write things down, and apply the same growth rules everywhere.

Then the Italian hire stops being a remote exception and becomes a core part of the team.
