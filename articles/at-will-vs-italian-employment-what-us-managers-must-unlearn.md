# At-will vs Italian employment: what US managers must unlearn

> At-will habits break in Italy. Learn how dismissal, notice, collective agreements and documentation change how a US manager hires, manages and exits staff.



If you manage in the US, you carry a default setting: either side can end the job at any time, for almost any reason. In Italy that setting does not exist. Most of the friction US teams hit comes from managing with the wrong default.

This article covers the mindset shifts. It is not legal advice. Your Italian counsel or employer of record should confirm the details for your case.

## 1. Employment is the default, not a favor

In the US, the burden sits with the employee. They stay as long as the company wants them.

In Italy, the burden sits with the employer. A permanent contract is the normal form of employment. Ending it needs a recognized reason, and you must be able to prove that reason if the employee challenges it.

So stop asking, "Do we still want this person?" Start asking, "Do we have a documented reason that Italian law accepts?"

## 2. "Not a fit" is not a reason

US managers often exit people for fit, attitude, or a change in strategy. Those words carry little weight in Italy.

Italian law looks for specific grounds. Broadly, these are serious misconduct, objective business reasons, or a subjective failure serious enough to justify ending the relationship. Each has its own standard. Each needs facts, dates, and records.

If a role is no longer needed, you must show that the need is real. You may also have to show you considered other options inside the company. A reorganization announced in a slide deck is not enough.

## 3. Documentation is the real management tool

In an at-will setting, a manager can act first and write later. In Italy the order flips.

Performance and conduct problems need a paper trail built over time. Clear written expectations, written feedback, and a record of support offered all matter. When a dispute arrives, a judge reads your file, not your intentions.

This changes daily habits. A quick Slack message saying "this is not working" does not count as a process. Your HR lead needs to own a consistent method for recording issues.

## 4. Discipline follows a procedure

For misconduct, you cannot simply tell someone to leave. The employee has the right to know the charge in writing and to respond before any sanction. Skipping that step can undo an otherwise valid decision.

Timing matters too. Act too slowly and you may be seen as having tolerated the behavior. Act too fast and you deny the right to respond. Managers need to learn this rhythm before they need it.

## 5. The collective agreement sits above your offer letter

US offer letters are built from scratch around the company's preferences. In Italy, most employees fall under a national collective agreement (CCNL). It sets minimum pay levels, job classifications, working time, leave, notice periods, and more.

You cannot offer less than the agreement says. You also cannot ignore the classification system when you promote someone or change their duties. Which agreement applies depends on the sector and the work, and picking the wrong one is a common early mistake.

Expect the agreement to answer many questions you would normally settle in negotiation.

## 6. Notice runs both ways, and it is not a courtesy

At-will teams often treat notice as optional. In Italy, notice periods are set by the applicable agreement and are part of the deal. If you end a contract without a valid reason, or skip the required notice, you create a cost.

Employees must also give notice when they resign, so planning handovers is easier than in a pure at-will setting.

## 7. Probation exists, but it is not a loophole

A probation period can be agreed in writing at the start. It gives both sides more freedom to part ways. But it only works if it is set up correctly and the employee is actually evaluated on the real duties. A vague or late clause can fall apart. Treat probation as a real review window with clear goals, not as a shortcut.

## 8. Remedies are different

In the US, a wrongful termination claim is rare and narrow. In Italy, an unjustified dismissal can lead to financial compensation, and in some situations reinstatement. The outcome depends on the type of defect and on the company.

The practical point is simple. A bad exit is not a quiet settlement. It can become a long, public, and expensive dispute.

## 9. Change is slower, so hire more carefully

Because exits take care, hiring decisions carry more weight. Define the role in writing. Check the classification. Use a structured interview process. Agree the probation terms. Many problems that look like firing problems are really hiring problems.

## What to do on day one

- Name one owner for employment records.
- Confirm the correct collective agreement before making an offer.
- Write job descriptions that match the classification.
- Give feedback in writing, early and often.
- Ask your local advisor before any exit conversation, not after.

## The shift in one sentence

In the US, you manage by freedom to leave. In Italy, you manage by obligation to justify. Teams that accept this early hire better, document better, and rarely face the dispute they feared.
