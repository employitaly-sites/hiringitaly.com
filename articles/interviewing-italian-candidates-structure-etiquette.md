# Interviewing Italian Candidates: Structure and Etiquette That Build Trust

> How US teams can run interviews with Italian candidates: what to expect, how to structure each stage, and the small courtesies that build trust early.



Hiring in Italy starts with an interview that feels different from the US version. The format is not hard. But a few cultural habits change how candidates read you. Get them right and you earn trust before any offer is made.

## Expect a more relational process

Italian candidates often judge the employer as much as you judge them. They want to know who they will work for, how the team treats people, and whether the company will still be around in a few years. A foreign company with no local presence raises extra questions. Answer them before they are asked.

Open the call with a real introduction. Say who you are, what the company does, and why you are hiring in Italy. Explain how the hire will be employed and paid. A short, clear note about your employment setup removes a lot of worry.

## Greet properly and start formal

Use the candidate's surname and a title at the start, unless they invite first names. Many Italians use a formal register with strangers. If your candidate switches to first names, follow them.

A few small points help:

- Send the agenda and the names of the interviewers ahead of time.
- Start on time and say hello to everyone on the call.
- Allow a little general conversation before the questions begin.

Skipping small talk can read as cold. You do not need long chats. A few friendly lines are enough.

## Structure the conversation

A clear shape helps both sides, especially when the interview is in a second language.

**Introduction.** Present the company and the role. Let the candidate see the whole picture before they speak.

**Background.** Ask the candidate to walk through their career. Italian candidates often give context about their studies and earlier employers. Let them. Education and path matter in Italy, and it is part of how they show who they are.

**Role-specific questions.** Ask for concrete examples of past work. Follow up on details. Avoid trick questions and brainteasers. They tend to damage trust.

**Their questions.** Leave real room for them. Expect questions about team, growth, working hours, and stability.

**Next steps.** Say what happens after the call and when they will hear from you. Then keep that promise.

## Language and tone

If the role needs English, test it in the interview, but be kind about it. Speak slowly, avoid idioms, and repeat key points in plain words. Many strong candidates are less fluent in a fast-paced call than in daily work.

In Italy, modesty and directness can sit side by side. Some candidates will not oversell themselves. Others will speak at length about the team and not about their own wins. Ask follow-up questions like "What was your part in that?" to get the detail you need.

## Use video well

Most first interviews run on video. Keep your camera on, test your audio, and pick a quiet space. Be ready to switch to a phone call if the connection fails, and do not make the candidate feel at fault.

For senior roles, an in-person or in-office meeting can be a strong signal of commitment. If you cannot travel, bring a local contact or a senior leader on the call. Seeing a real person from the company helps.

## Involve the right people

Candidates like to meet their future manager. Do not let the process sit only with HR or a recruiter. A founder or hiring manager on the call shows the role matters.

Keep the number of rounds reasonable. A long, unclear process makes people think the company is disorganized. Tell candidates the full process at the start.

## Talk about pay and terms openly

Be ready to discuss compensation clearly. Italian candidates think in terms of gross annual pay, and often also in terms of extras such as additional monthly payments, benefits, and time off. Do not leave them to guess. If you work with an Employer of Record (EOR), explain that it handles payroll and compliance locally, so the candidate knows who issues the contract and the payslip.

Avoid pressure tactics. Exploding offers and vague promises hurt your reputation fast. Italian professional circles are small, and people talk.

## Follow up with care

Send a short thank-you message after the call. Give feedback if you reject someone, even briefly. A polite, honest no is remembered, and rejected candidates often refer others to you later.

If you make an offer, put it in writing, explain each element, and give time to decide. Candidates may want to talk it over with family or a trusted adviser. That is normal.

## What to remember

Be punctual, prepared, and warm. Explain your setup in Italy. Respect formality at the start. Give clear structure, honest answers, and a fair timeline. These habits cost nothing, and they separate employers people want to join from those they doubt.
