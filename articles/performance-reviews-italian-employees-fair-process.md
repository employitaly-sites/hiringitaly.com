# Performance Reviews for Italian Employees: A Fair Process

> How to run performance reviews in Italy: clear criteria, written records, employee response and links to the CCNL. A practical framework for US teams.



US performance reviews often rest on manager judgment, a rating and a quick conversation. In Italy that approach can create problems. Employees expect a clear process. Records matter if a dispute ever arises. This guide gives a framework that fits both.

## Why Italy is different

Italian employment is built on written rules and documented fairness. Dismissal needs a justified reason, and poor performance is hard to prove after the fact. A vague review file will not help you. A clean record of expectations, feedback and support will.

The national collective agreement (CCNL) that applies to your employee also matters. It can set job levels, duties and disciplinary steps. Your review process should respect it, not sit beside it.

Employees also tend to expect a direct and honest conversation, with reasons given. A rating with no explanation feels arbitrary and reduces trust.

## Start with the job, not the score

A fair review needs a fixed reference point. Before any review:

- Check the employment contract and the job level under the CCNL.
- Write down the duties and goals for the period.
- Make sure the employee has seen and acknowledged them.

If you rate someone against goals they never received, the review has no weight. Goals should be specific and within the person's control. They should also match the role the contract describes. Assigning duties far outside the contracted level can create its own legal risk.

## A review cycle that works

### Set expectations in writing

At the start of the period, send the goals by email or through your HR system. Ask the employee to confirm receipt. Keep this confirmation.

### Hold regular check-ins

Short, frequent conversations beat one annual surprise. After each, send a brief written summary. Two sentences are enough: what was discussed, what was agreed. This builds a trail that shows steady feedback and real support.

### Run the formal review

Use the same template for everyone in similar roles. Cover:

- Results against the agreed goals.
- Specific examples, not general impressions.
- Strengths and gaps.
- Support or training offered.
- Goals for the next period.

Share the draft before the meeting if you can. Italian employees often value time to prepare.

### Let the employee respond

Give space for comments in the form itself. Ask the employee to add their view in writing and to sign that they received the document. A signature should confirm receipt, not agreement. Say that clearly.

### Keep the file

Store the signed review, the goals and the check-in notes together. Limit access to people who need it. Employee data is covered by European privacy rules, so keep only what you need and be able to explain why.

## Keep reviews separate from discipline

A review is a development tool. A disciplinary process is a formal procedure with its own rules. In Italy the disciplinary route usually requires a written notice of the issue, a chance for the employee to respond and a reasoned decision. The CCNL often adds details.

Do not use a review meeting to deliver a sanction. Do not call a conversation a review and then treat it as a warning. If performance problems are serious, speak with local counsel or your employer of record before you act. The order of steps matters.

## Be careful with tools and data

Many US teams use dashboards, activity trackers and automated scoring. In Italy, tools that can monitor employees raise rules on consent, union agreement and privacy. Using output metrics that come from normal work is safer than tracking behavior. Before you roll out any measurement system, check that it is allowed and that employees have been properly informed.

## Avoid common mistakes

- **Surprise ratings.** If the employee hears the problem for the first time at the review, the process failed.
- **Forced ranking.** Comparing people against each other fits poorly with an Italian framework of fixed duties and fair treatment.
- **Bias and unequal treatment.** Keep criteria objective. Be careful with comments linked to age, sex, family status, health or other protected traits.
- **Linking pay to unclear ratings.** If a review affects pay or bonus, explain how. Check what the contract and CCNL already guarantee.
- **Missing records.** If it is not written down, it is hard to prove later.

## A short checklist

- Goals are written and acknowledged.
- Criteria match the contract and CCNL level.
- Check-ins are summarized in writing.
- The review uses a standard template.
- The employee can respond in writing.
- Records are stored securely.
- Discipline follows its own separate process.

## Final point

A fair review in Italy is a documented, two-way conversation tied to the real job. Done well, it supports development and protects the company. Done poorly, it gives you a record that does not help when you need it most.
