# Hiring in Food, Wine and Agritech in Italy: Talent and Seasonal Patterns

> How US teams can hire in Italy's food, wine and agritech sectors: where the talent sits, how the seasons shape hiring, and what to plan before you start.



Italy's food, wine and agritech sectors run on craft, land and the calendar. If you plan to hire there, you need to know where the people are and when they are available. This article covers both.

## Three sectors, three talent pools

Food, wine and agritech overlap, but they hire differently.

**Food production.** Italian food companies range from small family producers to large exporters. The talent here is practical. Quality managers, production supervisors, food technologists and export sales staff are the core roles. Many candidates have spent years inside one product category, such as cheese, cured meats, pasta or preserved goods. That depth is valuable. It also means they may be slow to move to a new category.

**Wine.** Wine talent is regional. Someone who knows Piedmont reds does not automatically know Sicilian whites. Winemakers, vineyard managers, cellar staff and export managers build their reputation in a specific area. Hiring across regions is possible, but expect a learning period. Export and brand roles are more portable, because they depend on languages and market knowledge more than on soil.

**Agritech.** This is the newest of the three. It mixes agronomy, sensors, data, software and machinery. Candidates come from agricultural universities, engineering schools and technical institutes. Many are strong in the field and weaker in commercial work. Others come from software and have never spent a day on a farm. The best hires often sit between the two.

## Where people are

Talent follows production. Food and wine clusters sit in the countryside and in mid-sized towns, not only in the big cities. Agritech is more spread out, with pockets near universities and research centers.

This matters for remote hiring. A field agronomist cannot work from a laptop in Milan. A product manager for an agritech platform can. Decide early which roles must be near the land and which can be based anywhere in Italy.

Some candidates will not want to relocate. Family ties to a region are strong. If you need someone on site, hire locally and do not expect people to move for you.

## The calendar drives everything

The agricultural year sets the pace for hiring. You will feel it even in office roles.

**Harvest.** Late summer and autumn are the busiest period for wine and for many food crops. Cellar teams, vineyard crews and logistics staff are stretched. Senior people are hard to reach and harder to interview. If you try to hire a winemaker or a production manager at harvest, expect delays. They will not leave in the middle of it.

**Winter.** Once the harvest is done, the pace drops. This is the best time to recruit for permanent roles. Candidates have time, and they are often thinking about their next move. Many sales and export professionals also plan their year at this point.

**Spring.** Growth, pruning and planting keep field teams busy. Agritech vendors see demand rise as farms prepare for the season. If you sell technology to growers, you want your team in place before this period, not during it.

**Summer.** Tourism pulls on the same labor market, especially in regions with wine tourism, farm stays and tasting rooms. Hospitality and direct-to-consumer roles compete with seasonal farm work. Some offices also slow down for the summer break, so plan interviews and start dates with that in mind.

## What this means for your hiring plan

Work backward from the season in which you need the person productive. If you need a vineyard manager ready for harvest, you should be hiring well before it. Notice periods, onboarding and local knowledge all take time.

Separate your roles into two groups.

- **Seasonal or field roles.** These depend on the calendar and on location. Plan early and keep your offer simple.
- **Year-round roles.** Sales, finance, operations, software and marketing can be filled in the quiet months and often remotely.

For US founders, the second group is usually where to start. You can build a core team in the off-season, then add field capacity as your footprint grows.

## Practical points for US teams

- **Language.** Technical and field roles are usually done in Italian. Export and software roles often work well in English. Check this before you write the job post.
- **Titles.** Italian job titles do not always match US ones. A "responsabile qualità" may carry more or less scope than a US quality director. Ask for a description of duties, not just a title.
- **Compensation.** Candidates compare the whole package, not only the base salary. Benefits and stability matter, especially in family-run firms. Your offer needs to hold up against that.
- **Local credibility.** In food and wine, trust is personal. A local hire with a good name opens doors that a foreign brand cannot. Give that person room to act.

## Employing without a local entity

Many foreign companies want to hire in Italy before they set up a company there. An Employer of Record (EOR) lets you employ people in Italy through a local employer, while you direct the daily work. This is useful when you test a market, or when you hire one agronomist or one export manager and do not want to build a full structure for it.

The key is to know the full cost before you make an offer. Salary is only part of it. Contributions and other employer costs add to the total.
