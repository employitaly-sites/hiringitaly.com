# Part-Time Contracts in Italy: Hours, Scheduling and Changes

> What an Italian part-time contract must state about hours and schedule, and how employers can change them later without creating legal risk.



Part-time work is common in Italy. It is also one of the easier contract types to get wrong. The reason is simple: the contract has to say much more about working time than a US offer letter does. And once it is signed, you cannot adjust the schedule on your own.

This guide covers what the contract must specify and how to change hours later.

## Put it in writing

A part-time contract should be in writing. Do not rely on an email thread or a verbal agreement about "roughly half days." The written contract is the document an inspector, a court or the employee will look at if there is a dispute.

If the working time is missing or vague, you invite problems. The employee can ask a court to fix the hours. The court may also treat the missing detail as a defect in the part-time arrangement. That can change the economics of the hire.

## What the contract must specify

The contract needs to describe the working time clearly. At a minimum, plan to state:

- The reduced hours, compared with full-time hours under the applicable national collective agreement (CCNL).
- How those hours are spread across the day, the week, the month or the year.
- The specific days and times when the employee works, or a clear rule for setting them.
- The rules for extra hours, if you want the option to ask for them.
- Whether any clause allows you to move the schedule, and under what conditions.

The point of the distribution requirement is predictability. An employee with a part-time contract often has another job, family duties or studies. They need to know when they are expected to work.

## Three common structures

Italian practice usually describes part-time in three forms.

**Horizontal.** The employee works fewer hours each working day. For example, mornings only, every weekday.

**Vertical.** The employee works full days, but only on some days of the week, month or year. For example, three full days each week.

**Mixed.** A combination of both. Some days are full, others are shorter.

Pick the structure that matches the real need and write it down. Do not leave this to a later conversation.

## The CCNL matters

The national collective agreement for your sector fills in many details. It may set limits on extra hours, rules for schedule changes, minimum notice and the pay for additional work. Two companies hiring the same role under different agreements can end up with different rules.

Before you draft anything, confirm which CCNL applies to the role. Then check what it says about part-time. Your contract should follow it, not repeat generic wording.

## Extra hours

Sometimes you need more time from a part-time employee without changing the contract. Italian rules distinguish between additional hours within the full-time limit and overtime beyond it. The details depend on the CCNL and the contract.

Three practical points apply:

1. Say in the contract whether you may ask for extra hours.
2. Check whether the employee's consent is needed each time.
3. Pay extra hours at the rate the CCNL requires, and record them in payroll.

Do not treat unrecorded extra hours as a favor. Unpaid work is a common source of claims.

## Changing the schedule later

This is where US teams are most often surprised. In the US, a manager can usually change a shift with a message. In Italy, the schedule in a part-time contract is part of the agreement. Changing it needs a legal basis.

There are generally three routes.

**Agreement with the employee.** The simplest path. Both sides agree to the new schedule and sign a written amendment. Keep a copy in the personnel file.

**A flexibility clause in the contract.** Some contracts include a clause that lets the employer move the time slot of the work, or extend its duration, within set limits. Such a clause has to be written clearly, and the CCNL may require specific safeguards. These usually include advance notice and some form of compensation for the employee. Without a valid clause, you cannot rely on this route.

**Statutory or contractual rights of the employee.** Certain employees have rights tied to their personal situation. These can include requests to move between full-time and part-time, or to change hours for health or family reasons. If an employee makes such a request, do not ignore it. Check the CCNL and get advice before you answer.

## Moving between full-time and part-time

A change in the number of hours is a change to the contract. Treat it as a formal amendment. Put the new hours, the new distribution and the start date in writing, and have both parties sign.

If you want to cut hours because business is slow, you generally cannot impose it. You need the employee's agreement or another valid basis. Proposing a reduction and a pay cut unilaterally is likely to be challenged.

## Payroll and benefits follow the hours

Pay for a part-time employee is proportional to the hours worked. Many benefits and contract terms also scale with the working time, though the exact treatment depends on the CCNL. Every change in hours therefore has a payroll effect. Notify your payroll provider before the change takes effect, not after.

## A short checklist

- Confirm the applicable CCNL.
- Choose horizontal, vertical or mixed.
- State the hours and their distribution in the contract.
- Decide whether you need extra hours and write the rule.
- Add a flexibility clause only if it meets the CCNL conditions.
- Document every later change in a signed amendment.
- Tell payroll about each change in advance.

## The practical takeaway

In Italy, the schedule is part of the deal. Specify it carefully at the start, and treat every later change as a contract matter, not a scheduling matter. That keeps both the employee and the company on solid ground.
