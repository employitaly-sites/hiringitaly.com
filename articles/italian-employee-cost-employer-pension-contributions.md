# What an Italian Employee Really Costs: The Employer Pension Share on Top of Gross Salary

> Employer pension contributions in Italy sit on top of gross pay. See the 23.81% share finance teams should add to an offer before setting a budget.



You agree a gross salary with a candidate in Italy. Finance builds a budget from that number. Months later the real cost is higher, and nobody can say why.

A large part of the gap is the employer pension contribution. It is easy to miss because it never shows up on the employee's payslip as a deduction. It is a separate charge on you.

This article isolates that one item. It does not cover every cost of an Italian hire. It shows how big the pension share is and how to put it in the budget from day one.

## Gross salary is not the cost of the hire

In the US, many teams think of salary as the base and benefits as the extras. In Italy, the employer's social security charge is a core part of the cost. It is set by rule, not negotiated, and it is calculated on the employee's taxable pay.

So an offer letter shows one number. The ledger shows that number plus the employer contributions. Finance needs to see both before the offer goes out.

## The employer pension rate

The main pension charge is called IVS. It covers invalidity, old age and survivors' benefits. The employer share is **23.81%** of taxable pay.

That is the employer side only. It is separate from what the employee pays out of their own gross salary. The employee's share reduces their take-home pay. Yours increases your cost.

The practical meaning is simple. Whatever gross salary you offer, add 23.81% of the taxable amount to get the cost of the pension line alone. This is before any other employer charge.

## Where budgets go wrong

We see the same mistakes again and again.

**Budgeting on gross only.** The hiring manager approves a salary band. Finance books exactly that. The pension charge is not in the plan.

**Treating it like a US payroll tax.** The structure is different. The base is taxable pay, and the rate is much higher than many US teams expect. A rough mental shortcut from US payroll will understate the cost.

**Forgetting it scales with every raise.** The contribution moves with taxable pay. A salary increase raises the pension charge with it. A bonus that counts as taxable pay can do the same.

**Comparing offers on the wrong basis.** If you compare an Italian hire with a hire elsewhere, compare total employer cost, not gross pay. Gross pay hides the difference.

## A note on the source

The 23.81% figure comes from a secondary source, a Italian tax and payroll information site. It is a reliable reference for planning. For a signed offer, have your payroll provider confirm the rate that applies to the specific role and contract. Rates can differ by employer category, so the confirmation step matters.

## Other employer charges to keep on the list

Pension is not the only employer-side cost. There is also the workplace injury insurance premium paid to INAIL. The rate tables from 2019 remain in force. The way they are applied changed in 2026, so the premium your employer account pays may not match what an older guide tells you.

We do not put a figure on that premium here, because it depends on the type of work and on your own record. The point for finance is to keep it as its own line in the model, next to pension. Do not fold it into a generic percentage.

## How to build the budget line

Use this order when you price an Italian hire:

1. Start with the gross salary you plan to offer.
2. Identify the taxable pay base.
3. Add the employer pension share of 23.81% of that base.
4. Add the injury insurance premium as a separate line.
5. Add the other employer charges your payroll provider lists.
6. Compare the total with the budget the hiring manager was given.

The gap between steps one and six is what finance needs to see. If the approved budget is close to the gross salary, the hire is under-funded from the first day.

## What to do before the offer

Show the hiring manager two numbers side by side: the gross salary and the full employer cost. Get approval on the second one. This avoids a renegotiation after the candidate has already accepted.

Also ask your payroll provider for a written cost breakdown by line. You want pension, injury insurance and the remaining items separated. That makes variance reviews much easier later.

The pension share is predictable. It is a fixed rate on a known base. The surprise only happens when it is left out of the plan.
