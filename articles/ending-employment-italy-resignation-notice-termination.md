# Ending Employment in Italy: Resignation, Notice and Termination Basics

> How employment ends in Italy: resignation, notice, dismissal and final pay. A plain overview for US founders, HR, finance and legal ops teams.



Hiring in Italy is the easy part for many US teams. Ending a job is where surprises show up. The rules are not hostile to employers, but they are formal. Process matters, and so does documentation.

This guide covers the basics. It is not legal advice for a specific case. It gives you the map so you ask the right questions early.

## The main ways employment ends

Most exits fall into a few groups:

- The employee resigns.
- The employer dismisses the employee.
- Both sides agree to part ways.
- A fixed-term contract reaches its end.
- The employee retires.

Each path has its own steps, costs and risks. Resignation is usually the simplest. Dismissal by the employer is the one that needs the most care.

## Resignation

An employee who wants to leave must follow a formal process. A casual email or a chat with a manager is not enough. Resignation is submitted through an official digital procedure, so the company can rely on a clear record that the choice was the employee's own.

After that, the employee works through the notice period. Notice is not a courtesy in Italy. It is a duty on both sides, and its length usually comes from the national collective agreement that applies to the role. The length can differ by sector, seniority and contract type. Your payroll provider or EOR should confirm the right period before you plan a handover.

If the employee leaves without serving notice, the employer can generally claim an indemnity in place of the missing notice. If the employer wants the person to stop working right away, it can often pay in place of notice. Check how your agreement handles this.

## Dismissal by the employer

Italy does not work like at-will employment in the US. An employer cannot simply end the relationship because it no longer wants the person. There has to be a recognized reason, and the employer has to be able to show it.

In broad terms, reasons fall into three types:

- **Misconduct.** Serious behavior by the employee that breaks trust. The employer must follow a formal disciplinary process, including a written charge and a chance for the employee to respond.
- **Business reasons.** An objective need linked to the organization, such as a role that no longer exists. The employer may need to show it cannot place the person elsewhere in the company.
- **Other grounds.** Some situations, such as long absence for illness beyond the allowed limit, have their own rules.

Also know what is off limits. Dismissal tied to discrimination, to pregnancy or family leave, or to an employee exercising a legal right is treated very seriously. Some of these cases are void, not just costly.

A dismissal must be communicated in writing. The letter should state the reason clearly when the law requires it. Vague or late letters are a common cause of lost disputes.

## Agreed exits

Many companies prefer a mutual agreement. Both sides sign terms for the exit, often with a payment on top of what is already owed. This can cut the risk of a later claim. To protect the company, the agreement often needs to be signed in a way the law recognizes, such as before a labor office or union representative. A private paper alone may not hold up.

## Fixed-term contracts

A fixed-term contract ends on its date, but the rules around renewals and extensions are strict. If you extend or renew too often, or without a valid reason where one is required, the contract can turn into a permanent one. Track end dates and decisions well in advance.

## What the employee is owed at the end

On exit, the final pay usually includes more than the last salary. Expect to settle:

- Unused vacation and leave.
- Accrued pro-rata items such as extra monthly salary payments, where the contract provides them.
- The end-of-service payment that builds up during employment in Italy.
- Any notice indemnity, if it applies.

Finance teams often miss the end-of-service payment because it does not exist in many US setups. It is a real liability that grows over time. Budget for it from the start.

## Practical steps for foreign employers

1. Read the applicable collective agreement before you hire. It drives notice and many exit terms.
2. Keep written records of performance issues and any warnings.
3. Do not tell an employee verbally that they are out. Use the formal steps.
4. Talk to local advisors before you start any dismissal, not after.
5. Plan the final pay run early, so nothing is left open.
6. Remember equipment, access and data. Close accounts on the exit date, not before the process is complete.

## The bottom line

Ending employment in Italy is manageable when you treat it as a process. Know the reason, follow the form, keep the paper trail and budget for the full cost of exit. Surprises come from skipping steps, not from the rules themselves.
