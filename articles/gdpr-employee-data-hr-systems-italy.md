# GDPR for Employee Data: What Your HR Systems Must Do for Italian Staff

> How US teams should handle Italian employee data in HR tools: collect less, avoid relying on consent, and set clear retention rules per data type.



You hired someone in Italy. Their data now sits in your HRIS, payroll tool, applicant tracker and shared drives. GDPR applies to all of it, and Italian practice adds its own layer.

This article covers three things your HR systems must handle well: data minimization, consent and retention. It is a practical guide, not legal advice.

## Why a US setup needs changes

Most US HR tools are built to collect everything and keep it. Italy expects the opposite. You hold personal data about employees only because you need it for a defined purpose. If you cannot name the purpose, you should not hold the data.

The rules apply because you process data of people in Italy, even if your servers and your headquarters are in the US. Your vendors count too. Each one that touches Italian staff data is a processor, and you need a proper agreement with each of them.

## Data minimization

Start with a data map. List every field you collect on an Italian employee and write down why you collect it.

Typical fields that are justified:

- Identity and tax code, needed for payroll and filings.
- Bank details, needed to pay salary.
- Contract terms, working time and role.
- Social security and tax data required by law.

Fields that often are not justified:

- Marital status or family details beyond what a benefit or tax deduction needs.
- Health information beyond what a sick-leave certificate or a legal obligation requires.
- Political, religious or union data. These are special categories and carry stricter rules.
- Full copies of identity documents kept in open folders.
- Social media profiles collected during hiring.

Check your HR tool's default forms. Many ask for fields you do not need. Turn them off or make them optional. If a field cannot be removed, restrict who can see it.

Access control is part of minimization. Payroll should see payroll data. A hiring manager in the US does not need to see medical certificates. Set roles so that each person sees only what the job requires.

## Consent is the wrong tool for most HR data

US teams often reach for a consent checkbox. In employment, this usually fails. Consent must be freely given. An employee who depends on you for their salary is not seen as free to refuse. Regulators in Europe have said this repeatedly.

Use other legal bases instead:

- **Contract.** Processing needed to run the employment relationship, such as paying salary.
- **Legal obligation.** Tax, social security and labor law duties.
- **Legitimate interest.** Narrow, documented cases, balanced against the employee's rights.

Consent still has a place in limited cases, such as an optional wellness program or a photo on a public website. It must be specific, optional and easy to withdraw, with no penalty for saying no.

Whatever the legal basis, you must tell employees what you do with their data. Give each Italian hire a clear privacy notice in plain language. It should state what you collect, why, who receives it, where it goes outside the EU, how long you keep it and what rights they have.

Transfers to the US need a valid mechanism. Check what your vendors and your own group rely on. Do not assume that a US cloud tool is covered.

## Monitoring tools need extra care

Italy is strict about tools that can monitor employees. Time trackers, laptop monitoring, location tracking and email scanning can trigger a requirement for union agreement or authorization from the labor authority before use. Ask local counsel before you switch on any feature that records employee activity. Switching it on by default in a global rollout is a common mistake.

## Retention

Keep data only as long as you have a reason. Then delete it or anonymize it.

Build a retention schedule by data category. For each one, record the reason and the rule that sets the period. Payroll and tax records follow legal retention duties. Recruitment data for candidates you did not hire should be kept briefly. Disciplinary and medical records follow their own rules.

Then make your systems enforce it:

- Set automatic deletion or archiving rules in the HR tool.
- Remove ex-employee accounts and shared-drive copies.
- Check backups and exports, which people forget.
- Log deletions so you can show what happened.

A schedule on paper that the software ignores will not help you in an audit.

## Employee rights

Employees can ask to see their data, correct it, and in some cases erase or restrict it. Your HR tools must be able to find all data on one person across systems. Decide who receives these requests and how fast you answer. Test the process before the first request arrives.

## Breaches

If employee data leaks, you may need to notify the Italian data protection authority within a short legal deadline, and sometimes the employees too. Have a named owner and a written plan. Include your vendors, since many breaches start on their side.

## A short checklist

- Map all Italian employee data and its purpose.
- Remove or restrict fields you do not need.
- Use contract and legal obligation as the base, not consent.
- Issue a privacy notice to every Italian hire.
- Sign data processing agreements with each vendor.
- Check transfer mechanisms for US storage and access.
- Review monitoring features before activating them.
- Set a retention schedule and automate it.
- Prepare for access requests and breaches.

An Employer of Record can take on much of this for the Italian side, since they hold the employment relationship and run local payroll. You still need to control what your own team sees and stores.
