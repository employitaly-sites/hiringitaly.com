# Managing a Distributed Team With Two Italians: Friction Points and Fixes

> A US manager adds two Italian hires to a distributed team. Here are the common friction points, from time zones to feedback, and practical fixes.



You run a distributed team from the US. Two of your newest people are in Italy. The work is fine. The friction shows up in the gaps: schedules, tone, expectations and who handles what.

Most of it is fixable. None of it needs a big program. It needs a few clear agreements, made early.

## 1. Time zones and meeting hours

The most common problem is also the simplest. Your Italian colleagues work in a very different part of the day than you do. A call that feels like a normal morning huddle for you can land at the end of their workday.

**Fix:** Pick a short overlap window and protect it. Keep recurring meetings inside it. Move everything else to written updates. Rotate the inconvenient slot if the team is spread across several zones, so the burden does not always fall on the same two people.

## 2. After-hours messages

A US manager sends a message in the evening. It arrives in Italy in the middle of the night or at dawn. If the team treats a fast reply as a sign of commitment, your Italians may feel pulled into a rhythm they did not agree to.

**Fix:** State the rule out loud. Messages outside the overlap window do not need an immediate reply. Use scheduled send. Mark anything truly urgent with a clear label and a phone call, not a chat ping.

## 3. Time off

Italian employees expect to take real time away, and they expect to be fully offline. Some will take long blocks at once. Managers used to shorter, split vacations sometimes read this as low availability.

**Fix:** Ask for time-off plans early and put them on a shared calendar. Plan deadlines around them. Do not ask people to check in while away. Cover the work with a named backup instead.

## 4. Feedback style

US feedback is often warm, frequent and framed with praise first. Italian colleagues may be more direct in a debate and more reserved about praise. Some will push back hard on an idea in a meeting and still be fully aligned afterward. Others will say little in a group and raise concerns in private.

Neither style is wrong. The risk is misreading them. You may take a vigorous argument as conflict. They may take mild US praise as vague.

**Fix:** Hold regular one-on-ones. Ask direct questions: what is blocking you, what would you change, what do you disagree with. Say plainly what good work looks like. Do not rely on tone alone.

## 5. Hierarchy and decisions

In some teams, people wait to be told what to do. In others, they decide and tell you later. Your Italian hires may expect a clearer chain of authority than your US team uses, or may be used to a more formal way of addressing seniors.

**Fix:** Write down who decides what. Make clear which decisions they can take alone and which need your sign-off. A short decision log removes most of the guessing.

## 6. Long meetings, thin notes

A distributed team that relies on meetings leaves the people in the awkward time zones behind. Decisions made in a call at the wrong hour are hard to challenge later.

**Fix:** Write first, meet second. Share a short brief before each meeting. Record decisions in writing right after. Let your Italian team members comment asynchronously and treat those comments as part of the decision.

## 7. Language

Your Italians likely work in English every day. Fluency varies, and nuance is the first thing to go in a second language, especially in fast calls and slang-heavy chat.

**Fix:** Avoid idioms and sports metaphors. Summarize in writing. Ask people to restate what they will do. Give them room to write in their own pace.

## 8. Contract and payroll questions

Employment terms in Italy differ from what a US manager expects. Questions about contracts, pay slips, leave and benefits will come up, and they will not look like US questions. If you handle each one yourself, you will slow down and risk giving wrong answers.

**Fix:** Do not improvise. Route all employment administration through one channel. An Employer of Record (EOR) can hold the Italian employment relationship and answer these questions, so you manage the work and not the paperwork. Tell your hires who to contact for what.

## 9. Feeling like an outsider

Two people in one country, far from a US-centered team, can drift. They miss hallway context. They hear about changes late.

**Fix:** Give them a buddy on the US side. Include them in planning, not just execution. Share company news in writing, in a place they can read at their own hour. Meet in person when you can.

## A short checklist

- Set a protected overlap window.
- Agree on after-hours rules.
- Plan around time off in advance.
- Hold regular one-on-ones with direct questions.
- Write down decision rights.
- Write first, meet second.
- Keep language simple and confirm understanding.
- Send all employment questions to one owner.
- Include them in the team, not just the task list.

Most friction is not about culture clashes. It is about unstated assumptions. Make the assumptions visible, and the team works.
