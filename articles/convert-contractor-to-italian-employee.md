# How to Convert a Contractor Into an Italian Employee

> A practical sequence for moving a freelancer in Italy onto payroll: what to check first, what to sign, and how to time the switch without gaps.



Many US companies start in Italy with a freelancer. It is fast and it works. Then the relationship grows. The person works set hours, uses your tools, reports to your managers and has no other clients. At that point the contractor setup is a risk, and moving the person onto payroll is the clean fix.

This guide covers the steps and the order to do them in.

## Why the switch matters

Italian authorities look at how the work is actually done, not at what the contract says. If the person is integrated into your team and takes direction from you, the arrangement can be reclassified as employment. That can bring back-pay of contributions and other claims. Converting on your own terms is cheaper and calmer than being forced to later.

It also helps the person. Employees get social security coverage, paid leave, sick pay and notice rights that a freelancer does not have. Frame the conversation around that.

## Step 1: Review the current relationship

Start with facts. Write down how the person works today.

- Who sets the schedule?
- Who assigns the tasks?
- Do they work only for you?
- Do they use your equipment and email?
- How long has the engagement lasted?

This tells you how urgent the change is. It also gives you a record of the starting point. Share it with your Italian counsel or provider before you draft anything.

## Step 2: Decide who will employ them

You have two main routes.

The first is to set up your own Italian entity and run payroll yourself. This makes sense when you plan to hire several people and want a long-term presence. It takes time to set up and needs ongoing local admin.

The second is to use an Employer of Record (EOR). The EOR becomes the legal employer in Italy. It handles the contract, payroll, contributions and compliance. You direct the daily work. This is usually the faster route for a single hire or a small team, and it lets you test the market before committing to an entity.

Pick the route first. Everything else depends on it.

## Step 3: Choose the contract terms

Italian employment is shaped by national collective agreements. The one that applies depends on your sector and the role. It sets minimum pay levels, job grades, working hours, leave and notice periods. You cannot go below it.

Work out three things:

- The correct collective agreement and job grade for the role
- Whether the contract is open-ended or fixed-term
- Whether a probation period applies and how long the agreement allows it

Open-ended is the normal default. Fixed-term contracts have limits and conditions, so ask before you choose one.

Expect the gross salary to be different from the old freelance fee. A freelancer pays their own taxes and contributions out of the fee. An employer pays additional contributions on top of salary. Build the new package from total employer cost, not from the old invoice amount.

## Step 4: End the contractor agreement

Do not run both arrangements at once. Overlap strengthens the case that the work was always employment.

Check the termination terms in the existing contract. Agree an end date with the contractor in writing. Settle the final invoices. Make clear in the document that the new employment contract replaces the old working relationship going forward.

The contractor may also need to close or pause their freelance tax registration. That is their decision, and they should speak to their accountant.

## Step 5: Complete the pre-hire formalities

Italy requires the employer to notify the authorities of a new hire before the person starts. The employment contract should be signed and in place before the first working day. The employer also registers the employee with the social security and workplace insurance bodies.

If the person is not an EU citizen, check their right to work before anything else. A permit tied to self-employment does not automatically cover employment. Your EOR or counsel can tell you what is needed.

## Step 6: Plan the timing

Work backwards from the start date you want.

First, settle the review and the choice of employer. Then agree the offer with the person. Then sign the employment contract and file the hire notification before day one. Set the contractor end date to the day before the employment start date, so there is no gap and no overlap.

If you use an EOR, much of this runs in parallel because the provider already has the entity and the payroll set up. If you open your own entity, expect the setup to be the longest part. Do not promise a start date to the person until the legal employer exists.

Pay attention to payroll cut-offs. Ask your provider when payroll closes, so the first payslip is correct and on time.

## Step 7: Onboard as an employee

On day one the person is an employee. Give them an employee handbook, a clear job description and a named manager. Confirm how leave, sick days and expenses work. Update their tools and access to match their new status.

Keep the paperwork from the whole change in one folder: the review, the signed termination, the contract and the filings. If anyone asks later, you can show a clean, well-timed transition.

## A short checklist

- Document how the person works today
- Choose your own entity or an EOR
- Set the collective agreement, grade and contract type
- Price the role on total employer cost
- End the freelance agreement in writing
- File the hire notification and register the employee before the start date
- Check work authorization for non-EU workers
- Align the end and start dates with no gap and no overlap

None of this is hard, but the order matters. Do it in sequence and the conversion is routine.
