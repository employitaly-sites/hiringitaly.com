# How to Monitor Employees in Italy Without Breaking Privacy Rules

> Which employee tracking tools are allowed in Italy, which need a union or labor office agreement, and what notice you must give your staff.



Many US companies bring their usual monitoring setup to Italy. Time trackers, laptop agents, GPS on vehicles, email scanning. Some of it is fine. Some of it can lead to fines and to evidence you cannot use in a dispute.

This guide explains where the line sits and what you need to do before you switch a tool on.

## The basic rule

Italy protects workers from remote control of their activity. Two layers apply at the same time.

- The Workers' Statute limits tools that can monitor employees, even if monitoring is not their main purpose.
- The GDPR and the Italian privacy code govern how you collect and use the personal data those tools produce.

You must satisfy both. Passing one does not excuse the other.

## What usually needs an agreement

If a tool is installed for organizational, production or safety needs, and it can also monitor workers, you need prior approval. You get it in one of two ways.

- A collective agreement with the works council or the union representatives in the company.
- Where there is no representation, or no deal, an authorization from the labor inspectorate.

Typical examples:

- CCTV cameras in workplaces.
- Software that logs activity, screens, keystrokes or application use.
- Systems that record location or movement through badges or sensors.
- Tools that scan email content or browsing history beyond basic security.

This step comes first. If you install the tool before you have the agreement or authorization, the setup is not compliant, even if you fix the paperwork later.

A foreign employer with a small team in Italy often has no union representation. In that case the labor inspectorate route is the normal path. Plan time for it before your start date.

## What does not need an agreement

The law makes an exception for tools that employees use to do their job, and for tools that record attendance and access. Examples:

- A company laptop and the standard business software on it.
- A company email account and phone used for work.
- Badge or clock-in systems that record arrival and departure.

This exception is narrower than it looks. A laptop is a work tool. A hidden agent on that laptop that captures screens is not. Once a tool goes beyond what the job needs, you are back in the agreement zone.

GPS is a common trap. A GPS device on a delivery van used to route and schedule work may need an agreement. A GPS app that tracks a salesperson all day for control purposes is harder to defend. Ask what the tool is for, and whether a lighter option does the same job.

## Notice is always required

Even when no agreement is needed, you must tell employees how monitoring works. Give them a clear written policy that covers:

- Which tools you use.
- What data they collect.
- How the data is used, and for what purposes.
- How long you keep it.
- Who can see it.
- How employees can exercise their data rights.

Write it in plain language. Give it to every employee in Italian, and keep proof that they received it. A policy buried in a handbook does not count.

If you want to use the data in a disciplinary matter, this notice matters a lot. Data collected without proper information may not be usable.

## Privacy principles you must follow

The GDPR sets the frame for every tool.

- **Legitimate purpose.** Define why you monitor. Security, safety and protecting company assets can qualify. General curiosity about productivity does not.
- **Proportionality.** Use the least intrusive method that meets the goal. Blanket, continuous monitoring is hard to justify.
- **Minimization.** Collect only what you need. Do not read private messages or personal browsing if a log of security events is enough.
- **Impact assessment.** Systematic monitoring of staff often calls for a data protection impact assessment. Do it before launch.
- **Retention limits.** Delete data when the purpose ends.

## Practical checklist before launch

1. List every tool that touches employee activity, including those bundled in your global IT stack.
2. Sort them into work tools, attendance tools and everything else.
3. For the "everything else" group, start the agreement or authorization process.
4. Switch off or limit features that are not needed in Italy. Many global platforms let you do this by region.
5. Draft the monitoring policy and the employee notice.
6. Run the impact assessment where required.
7. Brief your managers. They must not look at data outside the stated purpose.

## Remote and hybrid work

The same rules apply when people work from home. Home monitoring raises the privacy stakes, because the line between work and private life is thin. Webcam checks, screenshots and always-on tracking are high risk. Measure outcomes and deliverables instead of watching screens.

## What this means for a foreign company

Your headquarters policy probably does not fit Italy as it stands. Your global IT team may have rolled out tools that cannot be used there without an approval step. Check this before the first hire starts, not after.

When you hire through an employer of record, the local employer can help you align your tools and notices with Italian practice. You still need to decide what you monitor and why.
