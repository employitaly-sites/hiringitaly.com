# Managing Time-Zone Overlap Between the US and Italy

> How US teams work with Italian employees across the time gap: where meeting windows sit, which work goes async, and how to keep the setup fair.



Italy sits many hours ahead of the US. The gap is large enough that your day and your Italian employee's day barely touch. That is manageable. It only becomes a problem when nobody plans for it.

This guide covers where the overlap sits, how to use it, and which habits keep work moving when nobody is online together.

## Know where the overlap really is

The overlap is narrow. When your East Coast team starts its morning, your Italian colleague is already well into the afternoon. When your West Coast team logs on, Italy is close to the end of the working day or already finished.

This means the usable window is early for you and late for them. It also means East Coast teams have far more shared time than West Coast teams. Be honest about which one you are. A West Coast founder may have almost no live overlap on a normal day.

Two more details catch people out:

- The US and Europe change their clocks on different weekends. For a short stretch each year, the gap shifts. Meetings that were fine last week land an hour off.
- Italian public holidays and the common habit of long August breaks do not match the US calendar. Check before you schedule anything important.

## Protect the window, then spend it carefully

Treat the shared hours as scarce. Do not fill them with updates that could be a written note.

Good uses of live time:

- One-to-one conversations between manager and employee
- Hiring decisions and performance feedback
- Planning sessions where several people need to talk through trade-offs
- Sensitive topics where tone matters

Poor uses of live time:

- Status reports
- Reading documents aloud
- Approvals that need a yes or no
- Anything that fits in a short message

Put recurring meetings in the same slot every week. People plan their day around them. A fixed slot also shows respect for the person who has to stay late.

## Share the burden of awkward hours

If every call lands at the end of the Italian workday, resentment builds. Rotate the inconvenience. Some weeks the US side joins early. Other weeks the Italian side stays later. If one side always bends, say so openly and agree on what they get in return, such as time off in lieu or a later start the next day.

Also set a clear limit. Make it explicit that an Italian employee is not expected to answer messages in the evening because the US team is just starting its day. Italian labor rules and local culture put weight on rest and disconnection from work. A healthy norm is better than a rule you have to defend later.

## Build the async habit

Async work is not a fallback. In a split-time-zone team it is the main way work gets done. A few habits make it work.

**Write messages that can be answered without a follow-up.** State the context, the question, and what you need by when. A vague "quick question?" costs a full day when the other person is offline.

**Use a daily handoff note.** At the end of each person's day, they post what is done, what is blocked, and what the other side should pick up. The next person starts with the answer instead of a guess.

**Record decisions in writing.** After any live call, send a short summary with owners and next steps. The person who missed the meeting, or who was tired at the end of it, can act on it right away.

**Record short videos or screen captures.** For walkthroughs and reviews, a recording beats a meeting. The viewer can watch at their own pace and reply in writing.

**Set response expectations.** Say how fast you expect a reply for normal requests and for urgent ones. Define what urgent means. Agree on one channel for emergencies, and keep it rare.

## Make decisions without waiting

The biggest hidden cost of the time gap is waiting. A blocked question can burn a whole day in each direction.

Reduce that by giving your Italian employee clear decision rights. Agree on what they can approve alone, such as spending limits, customer replies, and small scope changes. Put it in writing. When they can act, the overlap window stops being a bottleneck.

For questions that do need your input, ask them to bring a recommendation, not just a problem. You can then reply with a short yes or no.

## Onboarding needs extra live time

The first weeks are the exception to the async rule. A new hire needs more live contact: introductions, context, and regular check-ins with the manager. Plan these early and expect them to use up much of the shared window. As the person gets settled, shift more of the work to written and recorded formats.

If a US founder is the direct manager, consider naming a local contact in Italy who can answer everyday questions during the Italian morning, when nobody in the US is awake.

## Quick checklist

- Fix a recurring overlap slot and keep it
- Rotate who takes the awkward hours
- Keep live time for talking, not reporting
- Use daily handoff notes and written decision summaries
- Define decision rights and response times
- Check holidays and clock changes before scheduling

Time zones do not stop a US company from running a strong Italian team. Clear habits do the work.
