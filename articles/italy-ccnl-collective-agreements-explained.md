# Italian CCNL explained: why every job maps to a collective agreement

> Every job in Italy falls under a national collective agreement (CCNL). Learn what it sets, why it matters, and how it shapes your hiring terms.



If you hire in Italy, you will meet the term CCNL early. It stands for Contratto Collettivo Nazionale di Lavoro, or national collective labor agreement. It is the document that sets the baseline terms for most employees in a given sector.

US teams often expect to write an employment contract from a blank page. In Italy, that is not how it works. The collective agreement already covers many of the terms. Your individual contract sits on top of it.

## What a CCNL is

A CCNL is negotiated between employer associations and trade unions for a specific sector. Commerce, manufacturing, tech services, logistics, hospitality and many others each have their own.

The agreement is not a law passed by parliament. It is a negotiated text. Yet in practice it acts as the reference standard for pay and conditions in that sector.

## What it sets

The CCNL covers the core of the employment relationship. Typical topics include:

- Job classification and levels
- Minimum pay for each level
- Working hours and overtime rules
- Paid leave and public holidays
- Notice periods when someone resigns or is dismissed
- Probation rules
- Sick leave and how it is supplemented
- Additional pay items, such as extra monthly payments, where the agreement provides them
- Training and union rights

These are the items US employers usually decide for themselves. In Italy, much of that decision is already made.

## Why every job maps to one

Think of the CCNL as a lookup. You start with the real work the person will do and the sector your business operates in. From that, you identify the agreement that applies. Then you place the role at a level inside it.

The level matters because it drives the minimum pay and many related terms. A junior analyst and a senior manager can sit under the same agreement. They will sit at different levels, with different minimums.

So the question is never whether a CCNL applies. The question is which one fits, and at what level the role belongs. Every employee ends up with an answer to both.

## Why it sets the baseline

The CCNL is a floor, not a ceiling. You can offer more than the agreement requires. You cannot offer less on the points it covers.

This has practical effects:

- **Salary.** Your offer must be at or above the minimum for the level. Many employers pay above it to attract talent.
- **Cost.** Leave, extra payments and notice rules all add to the real cost of an employee. The headline salary is only part of the picture.
- **Termination.** Notice terms come from the agreement. Planning an exit means reading it first.
- **Disputes.** If an employee challenges their treatment, the CCNL is usually the first document checked.

A contract that ignores the right agreement creates risk. The employee can claim the difference, and the gap can build over time.

## Choosing the right agreement

The choice is not based on your preference or on the cheapest option. It follows the sector of the business and the nature of the activity. A software company and a retail chain will not land on the same agreement, even if both hire an accountant.

There can also be more than one plausible agreement for a business. Some sectors have competing agreements signed by different organizations. Picking between them needs care. Check which one matches your activity, and confirm the terms before you issue an offer.

An error here is easy to make and slow to notice. It tends to appear later, in a payroll audit or a dispute.

## What this means for your hiring plan

For founders, HR, finance and legal ops teams, a few habits help:

1. **Map the role first.** Decide the sector, the agreement and the level before you set the salary.
2. **Read the agreement.** Do not rely on a summary from a different country's practice.
3. **Budget the full cost.** Include leave, extra payments and employer contributions, not just base pay.
4. **Put it in writing.** Your offer letter and contract should name the CCNL and the level.
5. **Recheck when roles change.** A promotion can change the level and the minimums that go with it.

## Why this helps you

The system can feel rigid at first. But it also gives you a clear starting point. Terms are known, comparable and public. You do not have to invent a leave policy or a notice period. You can plan against a fixed baseline, then decide where to offer more.

Once you know which CCNL applies, most of the structure of the hire follows from it.
