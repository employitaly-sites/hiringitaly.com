# Gross-to-employer cost: the same headline salary in Italy and in a US W-2 setup

> Who pays what on the same headline salary: employer and employee contributions and income tax in Italy, set against a US W-2 payroll.



A headline salary means something different in each country. In the US, the number on the offer letter is what the employer owes before payroll taxes and benefits. In Italy it is the same, but the split between employer cost, employee deductions and take-home pay works differently. This article puts the two side by side so finance and HR teams can see who pays what.

## Start with the same gross figure

Take one gross annual salary and apply it in both places. In both countries, that figure is not what lands in the employee's bank account. It is also not what the company spends.

There are three layers to compare:

1. Contributions paid by the employer on top of the gross salary.
2. Contributions withheld from the employee out of the gross salary.
3. Income tax withheld from the employee.

## The US W-2 picture

In a US W-2 setup, the employer pays its share of payroll taxes on top of the gross wage. The employee's share of payroll taxes is withheld from the gross wage. Federal income tax and any state income tax are withheld as well.

Health insurance is the big difference for most US teams. It is usually an employer-sponsored benefit, and the employer often pays a large part of the premium. That cost sits outside the salary figure, and it often gets added to the offer in negotiation.

So in the US, the employer's total cost is the gross wage plus employer payroll taxes plus benefits. The employee's net pay is the gross wage minus employee payroll taxes, income tax and any benefit contributions.

## The Italian picture

Italy follows the same three-layer logic. The percentages are different, and the system is more uniform because most of the cost is set by national rules rather than by employer choice.

### What the employer pays

The employer pays social security contributions to INPS on top of the gross salary. The main pension-related share, known as IVS (invalidity, old age and survivors), is 23.81% of the taxable base for the employer.

That is only one component. Other employer contributions apply as well, such as insurance against workplace accidents, and the exact mix depends on the sector and the collective agreement that covers the role. For a first estimate, the IVS share shows the order of magnitude. A full costing needs the other items added.

### What the employee pays

The employee's IVS contribution is 9.19% of the taxable base. It is withheld from the gross salary by the employer and paid to INPS. The employee never sees it as cash.

Compare the two rates. The employer's IVS share is far larger than the employee's. In the US, the employer and employee shares of payroll taxes are much closer to each other. This is why a US founder looking at an Italian offer often finds the total cost higher than expected, even when the gross salary looks familiar.

### Income tax: IRPEF

Italian personal income tax is called IRPEF. It is progressive, with three brackets:

- 23% on income up to 28,000 EUR
- 33% on income from 28,000 EUR to 50,000 EUR
- 43% on income above 50,000 EUR

The employer withholds IRPEF each month and pays it to the tax authority. The rate applies to each slice of income, not to the whole amount. A higher earner pays 23% on the first slice, 33% on the second and 43% only on the part above 50,000 EUR.

The employee's own pension contribution reduces the income that IRPEF is calculated on. Local surcharges and credits can change the final figure, so treat the brackets as the core of the calculation and not the whole of it.

## Side by side

| Item | Italy | US W-2 |
|---|---|---|
| Employer social contributions | Paid on top of gross; IVS share is 23.81% of the taxable base, plus other items | Employer payroll taxes on top of gross |
| Employee social contributions | 9.19% IVS, withheld from gross | Employee payroll taxes, withheld from gross |
| Income tax | IRPEF, progressive at 23%, 33% and 43% | Federal and state income tax, withheld |
| Health coverage | Largely through the national system | Usually employer-sponsored, a separate cost |
| Who remits to the authorities | Employer | Employer |

## What this means for an offer

Three points matter when you convert a US offer into an Italian one.

First, do not compare gross to gross and stop there. Compare total employer cost to total employer cost. In Italy, the employer contribution load is heavier. In the US, benefits carry part of that weight instead.

Second, the employee's take-home depends on the IRPEF brackets. A candidate who is used to US net pay will ask what the offer is worth after tax. Be ready to answer with a net figure, not only a gross one.

Third, the numbers vary by role. Sector, collective agreement and contract type all move the employer's cost. A single percentage never covers the whole picture, so run the real case before you commit to a salary.
