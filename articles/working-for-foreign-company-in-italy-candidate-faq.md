# Working for a Foreign Company in Italy: Answers for Candidates

> A plain FAQ HR teams can share with candidates who would work in Italy for a foreign employer: contract, pay, benefits, leave, tax and who to call.



Candidates in Italy often hesitate when the employer is based abroad. They worry about who actually employs them, which rules apply and who they can call when something goes wrong. This FAQ answers those questions in plain terms. HR can share it as is.

It is general information, not legal advice. Your contract and the collective agreement that applies to your role set the details.

## Who is my employer?

That depends on how the company hires in Italy. It may have its own Italian entity. It may use an Employer of Record (EOR). With an EOR, the EOR is your legal employer on paper. It signs your contract, runs payroll and handles social security. Your day-to-day work, goals and manager stay with the foreign company.

Either way, you get an Italian employment contract. Ask HR to tell you clearly which entity signs it.

## Will I have a proper Italian contract?

Yes. Employment in Italy is built on written contracts that follow Italian law. Most roles also fall under a national collective agreement (CCNL). It sets things like minimum pay, working hours, leave, notice periods and extra benefits. Ask which agreement applies to your role. It tells you a lot about what to expect.

## Does Italian labor law still protect me if the company is foreign?

Yes. If you work in Italy, Italian rules on working time, leave, termination and workplace safety apply. A foreign headquarters does not change that. The contract cannot take these protections away.

## Who pays my salary, and in what currency?

You are paid in euros into an Italian bank account. Payroll is run under Italian rules, whether by the Italian entity or by the EOR. You receive a payslip that shows your gross pay, deductions and net pay. Salary offers are often quoted as gross annual pay. Ask HR to confirm whether a number is gross or net, and how many payments per year the contract uses.

## What about taxes and social security?

The employer withholds income tax and social security contributions from your pay and sends them to the Italian authorities. You usually do not file the same way a freelancer does. Your payslip shows what was withheld. If you have other income or special circumstances, a local tax adviser can help. Your employer's payroll provider can explain how your own payslip is built.

## What benefits can I expect?

Italian employment comes with statutory protections, including health coverage through the national system, pension contributions and a severance fund that builds up while you work. Many contracts add benefits through the collective agreement. Companies can also offer meal vouchers, supplementary health insurance, equity or bonuses. Ask HR for a full list in writing.

## How does leave work?

You are entitled to paid annual leave, public holidays, sick leave and protected family leave. The exact amounts come from the law, your collective agreement and your contract. A foreign company's home policy does not replace these. If the company offers more generous leave than Italian rules require, the better terms apply.

## Can I work remotely?

Often yes, but agree it in writing. Remote work arrangements should state where you work, which equipment you get and how working hours are tracked. If you plan to work from another country for long periods, tell HR first. It can change your tax and social security position.

## What language will work be in?

Many foreign companies work in English. Your contract and official documents will usually be in Italian, sometimes with an English version. Ask for a copy in a language you are comfortable with before you sign, and ask which version prevails if they differ.

## What if I need a visa or work permit?

Citizens of the European Union can work in Italy without a permit. Other candidates may need authorization before they can start. Your employer should explain the process, who handles the paperwork and what the timeline looks like. Do not resign from your current job or relocate until the right to work is confirmed.

## Who do I call when I have a problem?

Ask this before you accept. You should know who handles payroll questions, who handles contract questions and who is your day-to-day manager. With an EOR, payroll and contract questions go to the EOR. Work questions go to your manager at the foreign company. A good employer can name these contacts on the first call.

## What happens if the arrangement ends?

Termination follows Italian rules, including notice and final settlement. If the company changes how it employs people in Italy, for example by opening its own entity, your rights should carry over. Ask HR how that transfer would work.

## What should I ask HR before signing?

- Which entity is my legal employer?
- Which collective agreement applies?
- Is the offered pay gross or net, and how is it paid out over the year?
- What benefits are included, and where are they written down?
- What are the notice terms?
- Who is my contact for payroll and contract issues?
- What are the remote work terms?

A clear answer to each question is a good sign. Vague answers are worth a follow-up.
