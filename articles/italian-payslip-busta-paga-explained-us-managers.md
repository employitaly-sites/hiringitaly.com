# The Italian Payslip (Busta Paga) Decoded for US Managers

> A section-by-section tour of the Italian busta paga, so US managers can answer employee questions about gross pay, deductions, leave and severance.



Your Italian employee brings you a payslip and asks why the net looks small. You open it and see a wall of Italian abbreviations. This guide walks through the document section by section, so you can answer most questions without calling payroll.

The payslip is called the *busta paga* or *cedolino*. It is a legal document. The employer must give it to the employee every pay period. It is usually dense, and layouts differ by payroll provider. The same blocks appear on all of them.

## 1. The header: who, where, what contract

The top of the page identifies the employer and the employee. You will see the employer's tax and social security registration data, the employee's tax code (*codice fiscale*), and the payroll period.

The key field here is the collective agreement, the *CCNL*. Italy sets many employment terms by sector-wide agreements. The CCNL defines the minimum pay for each job level, notice periods, leave rules and more. Next to it you will see the employee's *livello* (job level) and *qualifica* (category, such as employee, manager or executive).

If an employee asks why their salary "must" be at least a certain amount, the answer starts here. The CCNL and level set the floor.

## 2. Pay items: the gross side

This block lists everything the employee earns before deductions. Common lines:

- **Paga base or minimo tabellare.** The minimum pay set by the CCNL for that level.
- **Superminimo.** Pay above the CCNL minimum. This is where your negotiated salary usually shows up. It can be absorbed into later CCNL increases unless the contract says otherwise.
- **Scatti di anzianità.** Seniority increments, if the CCNL provides them.
- **Straordinari.** Overtime, paid at rates the CCNL sets.
- **Indennità.** Allowances, for example for travel, role or shifts.
- **Premi.** Bonuses.

The sum is the *totale competenze* or gross pay. Your offer letter number should reconcile with this total once you account for how your payroll provider annualizes it.

## 3. Time and leave

A separate block tracks days worked and leave balances. Italian employees accrue several types:

- **Ferie.** Paid vacation. The slip shows accrued, used and remaining balances.
- **ROL and ex-festività.** Reduced-hours leave and compensation for former holidays, depending on the CCNL. Not every contract has these.
- **Malattia.** Sick leave. Pay during sickness follows a mix of social security benefits and employer top-ups set by the CCNL.

Managers get many questions here. "How much vacation do I have left?" is answered by this block. Approving time off without checking it creates payroll corrections later.

## 4. Employee social security

Next come the deductions. The first is the employee's share of social security, paid to INPS. It is withheld from gross pay and sent to the authority by the employer.

This is different from the employer's own contributions. Those do not appear as a deduction. They sit on top of the gross salary and are a real cost to the company. Many US managers miss this. The payslip shows what the employee pays, not what the company pays in total. That is why the total cost of an Italian hire is higher than gross pay.

The result of this step is the *imponibile fiscale*, the taxable base for income tax.

## 5. Income tax withholding

The employer withholds income tax at source, much like US payroll. The lines to know:

- **IRPEF lorda.** Gross personal income tax, calculated on the taxable base using progressive brackets.
- **Detrazioni.** Tax credits that reduce the tax due. They depend on income level, family situation and dependents. Two employees with the same salary can have different net pay because of these.
- **Addizionale regionale and comunale.** Regional and municipal surcharges. They depend on where the employee lives, not where the company is.
- **Conguaglio.** An adjustment. It trues up the tax withheld against what is actually due, often at year end or when the employee's situation changes.

If an employee says their net changed with no salary change, check detrazioni and conguaglio first.

## 6. The net

At the bottom you find the *netto in busta*, the amount transferred to the employee's bank account. It is gross pay minus employee social security, income tax and surcharges, adjusted for credits and any other items such as union fees or salary advances.

## 7. TFR: the severance accrual

The slip also reports *TFR* (*trattamento di fine rapporto*). This is a severance amount that builds up during employment and is paid when the contract ends. It is not an extra deduction from the employee. It is a company liability that grows each period. The slip shows the accrual, and the employee may have chosen to direct it to a pension fund instead.

Expect questions here from employees who think the company holds back their money. Explain that TFR belongs to them and is paid at exit or moved to a pension fund.

## 8. Extra monthly payments

Many CCNLs provide additional salary payments during the year, often paid at set times. In Italy, this is part of normal annual compensation, not a bonus. When an employee asks why one payslip looks larger, check whether one of these payments falls in that period.

## How to use this

When an employee questions a payslip, work through it in order. Check the CCNL and level. Check the pay items. Check leave balances. Check deductions and credits. Then look at the net.

Most questions have a simple answer in one of these blocks. For anything involving a conguaglio or a legal interpretation, send it to your payroll provider.
