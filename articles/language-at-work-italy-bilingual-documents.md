# Language at Work in Italy: Which Documents Employees Must Get in Italian

> English can run daily work in Italy. Some documents still need Italian. Here is which ones to make bilingual, and why it protects your company.



Your Italian team may work in English every day. Meetings, Slack, product specs, performance talks. That is normal for a US company with a global team. But daily working language and legal document language are two different things.

This article explains which documents should be bilingual, and why a bilingual version is the safe default.

## The core principle

Italian employees live under Italian law. Italian courts and labor inspectors work in Italian. A document that an employee cannot read properly is hard to defend later. If a dispute arises, the question is often simple: did the employee understand what they signed or received?

An English-only document makes that question hard to answer in your favor. An Italian version, or a side-by-side bilingual one, makes it easy.

This is general guidance, not legal advice. Have Italian counsel confirm the details for your case, your sector, and your collective agreement.

## Documents that should be in Italian, or bilingual

**The employment contract.** This is the most important one. Give the employee a version in Italian. If you want English for your own records, put both languages side by side and state which one prevails. Italian is the safer choice for the prevailing text.

**The hiring notice and the terms of employment.** Employees must be told the key conditions of their job: role, pay, hours, place of work, notice, and the applicable collective agreement. Give this information in Italian so there is no doubt it was understood.

**Payslips.** Payslips follow Italian formats and Italian terms. Line items such as contributions, withholdings, and leave accruals have Italian names. Do not translate them away. If you want to help a foreign manager read them, add an English glossary. Keep the Italian payslip as the official one.

**Privacy notices and data consents.** Employee data is personal data. The notice that explains how you use it must be clear and easy to understand. A notice the employee cannot read is a weak notice. Provide it in Italian, and add English if useful.

**Health and safety information and training.** Safety rules protect people from real harm. Instructions must be understood to work. Provide materials and training in a language the employee fully understands. For Italian staff, that means Italian.

**Disciplinary notices.** If you ever need to raise a formal complaint or start a disciplinary process, the notice must be in Italian. These documents have strict form and timing rules. Do not improvise them in English. Involve counsel before sending anything.

**Termination documents.** Any notice of dismissal or resignation handling should be in Italian. The same goes for settlement agreements. These are the documents most likely to end up in front of a judge.

**Workplace policies that bind the employee.** Code of conduct, remote work rules, expense rules, IT use, and anything the employee must follow. If you want to enforce a rule, the employee should have received it in a language they read well.

## Documents where English alone is usually fine

Not everything needs translation. Internal material that does not create rights or duties can stay in English.

- Product and engineering documentation
- Team wiki pages and meeting notes
- Sales decks and customer material
- General company announcements
- Onboarding content about your tools and culture

If an English document starts to set a binding rule, move it to the first list.

## Why bilingual beats Italian only

Bilingual documents solve two problems at once.

First, the employee has the legally safe Italian text. Second, your US managers can read and approve what is being issued in their name. You avoid the risk of signing a contract nobody on your side can read.

A side-by-side layout works well. Italian on the left, English on the right. Add a clause that says which version prevails. Keep that clause the same across all employees.

## Practical rules

1. **Translate once, reuse often.** Build templates for each document type. Do not translate from scratch for every hire.
2. **Use a legal translator, not a quick machine pass.** Employment terms have precise meanings. A loose translation can change a right or a duty.
3. **Keep terms consistent.** Use the same Italian term for the same concept across all documents.
4. **Store both versions.** Keep the signed Italian version and the English reference copy together in the personnel file.
5. **Ask the employee.** Some senior hires prefer English only. Even then, keep an Italian version on file and let counsel advise whether to rely on the English one.
6. **Update both when the law or the collective agreement changes.** A stale translation is a hidden risk.

## Common mistakes

- Sending a US-style offer letter and calling it the contract.
- Relying on an employee's good English as proof they understood.
- Translating the contract but not the policies it refers to.
- Letting the English and Italian texts drift apart after edits.
- Using the English version for a disciplinary step because it is faster.

## The simple test

Ask one question about each document: if this ended up in front of an Italian judge, would the employee have been able to read and understand it? If the answer is not a clear yes, make an Italian version.
