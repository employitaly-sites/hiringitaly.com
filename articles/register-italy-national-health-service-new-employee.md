# How New Employees in Italy Register with the National Health Service

> How employees and their families sign up for Italy's public healthcare: what to bring, who handles it, and what to expect once enrolled.



Italy has a public health system called the Servizio Sanitario Nazionale, or SSN. Employees who work in Italy pay into it through payroll contributions. In return, they and their families can use public care. For a US company hiring its first person in Italy, this is one of the first practical questions the new hire will ask.

This article explains how registration works, what the employer does, and what families should expect.

## What the SSN covers

The SSN gives access to a family doctor, specialist visits, hospital care, emergency treatment, and many diagnostic tests. Some services are free at the point of use. Others carry a small co-payment, known as a ticket. What you pay depends on the service, the region, and the person's circumstances.

Healthcare in Italy is run largely at the regional level. The basic framework is national, but the booking systems, forms and waiting times differ from one region to another. A new hire in Milan may see a different process than one in Naples or Palermo.

## Who is eligible

Employees working legally in Italy are generally entitled to enroll. This covers Italian citizens, EU citizens and non-EU nationals who hold a valid residence permit for work. Enrollment is tied to legal residence and employment, not to citizenship.

Family members can usually be enrolled as dependents. This typically includes a spouse or partner and children. Non-EU families need their own valid residence documents. The exact paperwork depends on the family's status, so it is worth checking with the local office early.

## The documents you need

Before anything else, the employee needs a tax code, called the codice fiscale. Italy uses it for almost every administrative step, including health registration. Many new hires get it early in the onboarding process.

The usual documents are:

- A valid passport or identity card.
- The codice fiscale.
- The employment contract or proof of employment.
- A residence permit, for non-EU nationals.
- Proof of address in Italy, such as a lease or a residency registration.
- Documents showing family ties, if dependents will be enrolled.

Non-EU families may need translated and legalized civil records. Ask the local office what it will accept.

## Where to register

Registration happens at the local health authority, the Azienda Sanitaria Locale, usually shortened to ASL. Each area of Italy has its own. The employee registers with the ASL that covers their place of residence.

Many offices let you start online. Others require an in-person visit. Some accept an appointment booking only. Check the website of the relevant ASL before going.

At registration, the employee is entered in the system. The ASL then issues a health card, the tessera sanitaria. The card carries the person's tax code. It is used at pharmacies, clinics and labs.

## Choosing a family doctor

After enrollment, the employee picks a family doctor, the medico di base. Children generally get a pediatrician instead. The choice is made from a list of doctors in the area who are accepting patients.

The family doctor is the main contact for everyday care. They treat common illnesses, write prescriptions, and refer patients to specialists. In most cases, you need this referral to book a specialist visit under the public system.

A good family doctor can make the system much easier to use. New residents should not be afraid to switch if the fit is poor.

## Using the system day to day

Once enrolled, a typical visit works like this:

- The patient books an appointment with the family doctor.
- The doctor issues a prescription or referral, often in electronic form.
- The patient books the specialist or test through the regional booking system, or at a pharmacy that offers the service.
- The patient pays any co-payment and attends the visit.

Pharmacies play a bigger role in Italy than many Americans expect. They give advice, fill prescriptions, and often handle bookings.

For emergencies, the hospital emergency room, the pronto soccorso, is open to everyone. Triage decides who is seen first.

## Waiting times and private care

Public care has real limits. Waiting times for some specialist visits and tests can be long, especially in busy regions. Many Italians use private clinics for faster access. Some employers offer supplementary health insurance as a benefit, which can cover private visits and reduce waiting.

This is a point worth raising with new hires from the US. They may expect an insurance card and a short wait. The Italian model works differently. It is broad and affordable, but it asks for patience and paperwork.

## What the employer should do

The employer does not register the employee with the SSN directly. But a good employer makes the process easier.

- Help the employee get the codice fiscale before the start date.
- Provide a clear employment contract and proof of employment.
- Explain that payroll contributions fund the public system.
- Share a short guide to the local ASL and how to book.
- Consider supplementary insurance for faster private access.

This support has a real effect on retention. A new hire who feels lost with healthcare in the first weeks is more likely to feel unsettled overall. This matters most for people relocating with a family.

## Common mistakes

The most common problem is delay. Employees wait to register until they are sick. Then they find that the card and the doctor assignment take time. Start early.

Another mistake is assuming the process is the same everywhere. It is not. Always check the rules of the specific region and ASL.

A third is forgetting dependents. Families should be enrolled together, so children and partners can access care from the start.

## In short

Italy's public health system is open to employees and their families. Registration needs a tax code, valid documents and a visit to the local ASL. After that, a family doctor becomes the entry point to care. Employers cannot do the registration for the employee, but they can remove most of the friction.
