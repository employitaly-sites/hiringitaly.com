Year-end in a US company usually means bonus approvals, review cycles and a payroll cleanup. With Italian employees, the same tasks follow different rules. Some deadlines come earlier than you expect. Some payments you never planned for are already owed.

This checklist covers the four areas that cause the most trouble: bonuses, leave balances, reviews and data. Use it as a starting point for a conversation with your payroll provider or employer of record.

Bonuses and extra pay  

Start with what is already promised. Italian employment contracts sit under a national collective agreement (CCNL). Many of these agreements require extra salary payments on top of the regular monthly pay. Your US team may not know they exist. If you hired through an offer letter written in the US style, check that these payments were budgeted.

Then look at discretionary bonuses.

  • Confirm whether the bonus is contractual or truly discretionary. If it was paid repeatedly and employees expect it, it may be treated as part of pay.
  • Decide the amount and the payment month before the payroll cut-off, not after.
  • Ask how the bonus is taxed and what social contributions apply. The employer cost is higher than the amount the employee sees.
  • Keep a written record of the criteria. Vague criteria lead to disputes.

A US-style equity or stock grant needs its own review. Tax and reporting treatment in Italy can differ from the US, for both the employee and the employer. Do not wait until the vesting date to find out.

Leave balances  

US companies often treat unused vacation as use-it-or-lose-it, or they pay it out under a policy. Italy works differently. Employees have a statutory right to paid leave, and unused leave does not simply disappear because the calendar turns.

Before year-end:

  • Pull the current leave balance for each Italian employee.
  • Check what your CCNL and the employment contract say about carry-over.
  • Look at other kinds of paid time off, such as permits and special leave. These are often tracked separately from vacation.
  • Plan time off with managers now. Leave pushed into the new year builds up and creates cost and scheduling problems later.

If an employee leaves, accrued leave and other deferred pay are settled in the final payment. Track them throughout the year so the figure is never a surprise.

Performance reviews  

Reviews are fine in Italy. The risk is in how they are used.

  • Keep reviews factual and tied to the role and agreed goals.
  • Do not use review scores as the sole basis for a pay cut or a demotion. Changes to duties or pay need a proper legal process.
  • Write down feedback and share it with the employee. Documented, consistent feedback protects both sides if a dispute ever arises.
  • Keep forced ranking and anonymous peer scoring under review. They can raise data protection and fairness questions.
  • If a raise follows the review, check whether the collective agreement sets minimum pay levels for the role. A raise may be needed just to stay compliant.

Deliver the review in a language the employee is comfortable with. A rushed video call from a different time zone is not a good setting for hard feedback.

Employee data  

Year-end is when HR data gets exported, copied and emailed. For Italian employees, that data falls under EU data protection rules.

  • Limit who in the US can see Italian payroll files, review notes and medical or leave information.
  • Use secure systems, not email attachments or shared spreadsheets.
  • Make sure employees know what data you hold and why. Update notices if you added a new HR tool this year.
  • Check that the legal basis for transferring data to the US is in place.
  • Delete data you no longer need, according to a retention rule you can explain.

Also confirm that basic records are current: address, tax details, bank account, emergency contact and any change in family status. Errors here lead to payroll corrections in the new year.

Other items to close out  

  • Review each employee’s contract against actual duties. Drift over the year is common.
  • Check that expense reimbursements and benefits are documented and processed correctly.
  • Confirm any filings and payments your payroll provider needs from you before the holiday period. Italian offices slow down around year-end, so late requests wait.
  • Set the budget for next year with the full employer cost in mind, not just base salary.

A simple way to work  

Do not try to handle all of this in one meeting. Assign each area to one owner. Finance takes bonuses and budget. HR takes leave and reviews. Legal or your provider takes data and contract checks. Set a date for each, and leave buffer time before payroll closes.

Most year-end problems in Italy come from assuming US practice applies. A short review now costs far less than a correction later.
