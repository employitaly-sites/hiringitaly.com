# Junior vs Senior Engineer in Italy: How Net Pay and Employer Cost Differ

> How Italian income tax brackets and the employment tax credit change net pay between junior and senior engineers, and why employer cost scales differently.



If you hire engineers in Italy, you will see a pattern fast. A senior engineer costs you more, but the senior's take-home pay does not rise in step with that cost. The reason sits in two parts of Italian payroll: progressive income tax and a tax credit for employees.

This article explains how both work, and why the gap between employer cost and net pay widens with seniority.

## Two different mechanics

Employer cost and net pay follow different rules.

Employer cost is mostly proportional. The pension-related contribution paid by the employer (IVS) is 23.81% of taxable pay. Double the gross salary and this part doubles too. There is no bracket and no cap in the facts we use here. So the employer side scales in a straight line with seniority.

Net pay is not proportional. It depends on income tax (IRPEF), which is progressive, and on a tax credit that shrinks as pay grows. That is where seniority changes the picture.

## The progressive brackets

Italian income tax applies rates in slices of annual income:

- Up to €28,000: 23%
- From €28,000 to €50,000: 33%
- Above €50,000: 43%

These rates apply to the slice, not to the whole salary. A senior engineer whose pay crosses €28,000 pays 33% only on the part above that line. The same logic applies at €50,000.

Still, each extra euro earned by a senior engineer is taxed at a higher rate than each extra euro earned by a junior. That is the first reason net pay grows more slowly than gross pay.

## The employment tax credit

The second factor is the tax credit for employees (detrazione per lavoro dipendente). It reduces the income tax due. The amount depends on income, and it falls as income rises.

The formula by income level (R) works like this:

- Income up to €15,000: a credit of €1,955. The minimum is €690. For fixed-term contracts it is €1,380.
- From €15,000 to €28,000: €1,910 plus €1,190 times (28,000 minus R) divided by 13,000.
- From €28,000 to €50,000: €1,910 times (50,000 minus R) divided by 22,000.
- An extra €65 applies for income between €25,000 and €35,000.
- Above €50,000: no credit.

Read this from the top. A junior engineer near the lower end gets the largest credit. As pay moves up, the credit shrinks step by step. Once a senior engineer passes €50,000, it is gone.

## What this means for a junior hire

A junior engineer is more likely to sit in the first bracket at 23%. The credit is still large or only partly reduced. The tax bill is lighter, and more of each gross euro reaches the employee.

For you, the employer cost stays simple: gross pay plus the employer contribution at 23.81% of taxable pay. For the employee, the credit softens the tax bill. This is why a junior offer can look generous in net terms compared with its cost to you.

## What this means for a senior hire

A senior engineer is more likely to cross €28,000 and, in many tech roles, €50,000. Two things happen together.

1. Marginal income is taxed at 33%, then at 43%.
2. The tax credit declines, then disappears.

Both effects reduce net pay at the same time. The credit shrinks across the 28,000 to 50,000 band, so the effective tax rate in that band is higher than the headline 33% suggests. Above €50,000 the credit is zero and the 43% rate applies to the top slice.

Meanwhile your employer contribution keeps rising in a straight line at 23.81% of taxable pay. So each step up in seniority adds the full employer cost, but gives the employee a smaller share of it as net pay.

## Why this matters for offers

Three practical points for founders and finance teams:

- **Do not compare offers by gross pay alone.** A candidate thinks in net pay. You think in total employer cost. The two diverge as salary rises.
- **Expect pushback on senior offers.** A senior candidate may see a smaller net gain from a raise than expected. That is the bracket and credit effect, not a payroll error.
- **Model each hire separately.** The credit formula depends on exact income, and the contract type matters at the lower end, since fixed-term contracts use a different credit figure.

These figures are based on secondary sources and cover only income tax, the credit and the employer pension contribution. Employee social contributions and local taxes also affect net pay, so confirm final numbers with your payroll provider before you send an offer.

## The short version

Employer cost rises in a straight line with gross pay. Net pay rises more slowly, because higher brackets and a disappearing tax credit take a bigger share as seniority grows. Juniors keep more of each euro. Seniors keep less of each extra euro.
