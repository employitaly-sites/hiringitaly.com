# Parental Leave in Italy: How to Plan Around a Team Member's Absence

> A practical guide for US employers on planning team coverage, budget and handover when an employee in Italy goes on maternity or paternity leave.



When a team member in Italy announces a pregnancy or a new baby, the leave itself is rarely the hard part. The hard part is the plan around it. US teams often get caught out because they assume Italian leave works like leave at home. It does not. This guide covers how to prepare your team so the absence is calm and predictable.

## Start with the right expectations

Italy protects parental leave strongly. Mothers have a mandatory period of leave around the birth. Fathers have their own leave rights too. Beyond that, parents can take further optional leave over the following years.

For planning, this means three things:

- The leave is a legal right, not a favor. You cannot negotiate it down.
- The employee is protected from dismissal during a protected period. Do not plan a restructure around the absence.
- The role stays theirs. You are covering a position, not replacing a person.

Confirm the exact entitlements for your employee's contract and collective agreement before you set dates. Rules differ by situation, and your payroll or EOR provider should give you the current numbers in writing.

## Talk early and keep the tone simple

Let the employee lead. Ask what timeline they expect and how much contact they want during leave. Some people want none. Some want occasional updates. Respect the answer and write it down.

Keep the first conversation short and practical:

- Thank them for telling you.
- Explain that you will plan coverage and want their input on the handover.
- Ask who handles their key tasks today.
- Agree on a date to review the plan together.

Do not ask about future plans for more children or about their intention to return. Those questions create legal risk and damage trust.

## Map the work, not the person

List everything the employee does in a typical month. Then sort each item into one of three groups:

- **Pause.** Work that can wait until they return.
- **Share.** Work that existing teammates can absorb in small pieces.
- **Cover.** Work that needs a dedicated person while they are away.

Most teams find the "cover" group is smaller than they feared. Be honest about what is truly urgent. If everything is urgent, nothing is prioritized and the team burns out.

## Decide how to cover the role

You have a few options, and they can be combined.

**Redistribute internally.** This works for short gaps and light workloads. Watch for overload. If you give a colleague extra work, adjust their goals and say so openly.

**Hire a fixed-term replacement.** Italian law allows fixed-term contracts for replacing an employee on leave. Use this route when the work needs a full-time owner. Make the purpose of the contract clear in writing. Plan for onboarding time, since a replacement needs a few weeks to become useful.

**Use a contractor for specific projects.** This suits defined deliverables. Be careful about using a contractor to do the same daily work as an employee. It can be reclassified as employment.

If you work through an Employer of Record, ask your provider to handle the replacement contract. They know the local rules and can move quickly.

## Budget for the overlap

The cost picture has more parts than founders expect. Your payroll cost for the absent employee does not always drop to zero, because social security institutions pay part of the leave allowance and employers may owe top-ups under some collective agreements. At the same time, you add the cost of the replacement.

Ask your payroll provider for a clear breakdown before the leave starts:

- What the employer pays during leave.
- What the social security institution pays.
- What any collective agreement adds.
- What a replacement will cost, including onboarding.

Put these numbers in your forecast early. Finance teams dislike surprises more than they dislike costs.

## Build the handover

A good handover is a document, not a conversation. Ask the employee to write down:

- Recurring tasks and where the files live.
- Key contacts, internal and external.
- Open projects and their status.
- Decisions that need an owner while they are away.

Schedule a walkthrough with whoever takes over. Record it if the employee agrees. Do this well before the leave starts, since timing can shift if the baby arrives early.

## Plan the return

The return deserves as much attention as the departure. Agree on a reconnection plan before leave begins:

- A short catch-up call before the first day, if the employee wants one.
- A light first week with a briefing on what changed.
- A check-in after a month to see how the workload feels.

Some employees may ask for reduced hours or flexible arrangements after leave. Italian rules give parents certain rights here. Treat these requests as part of normal planning and check the legal position with your provider.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Waiting until the last moment to plan coverage.
- Contacting the employee during leave without agreement.
- Treating the replacement as a permanent swap.
- Ignoring the cost until the first payroll run.
- Assuming US practices apply.

Good planning turns parental leave into an ordinary operational event. Your team stays steady, your employee feels supported, and you avoid legal trouble.
