# Expense Reimbursements in Italy: Per Diems, Receipts and Tax Treatment

> How travel and expense claims move through Italian payroll, and what decides whether a reimbursement is taxable or not.



Expense claims look simple until they hit payroll. In Italy, the way you pay a reimbursement decides whether it is taxed. It also decides whether it carries social contributions. A claim that is handled well costs the employee nothing. A claim that is handled badly can turn into taxable pay for the employee and extra contributions for you.

This article explains how the flow works and where US teams usually get it wrong.

## The basic principle

A reimbursement repays a real business cost. It is not pay for work. Italian payroll treats the two very differently.

If the employee spent money on behalf of the company and can prove it, the repayment is generally not part of taxable income. If the money is paid without proof, or paid as a fixed amount that does not match a rule, it can be treated as ordinary pay. Then it is taxed and contributions apply.

So the question is never just "was it a business expense?" The question is "can we document it in the way Italian rules expect?"

## Three ways to reimburse

Italian practice usually sorts travel and expense payments into three groups.

**Documented reimbursement.** The employee pays, keeps the receipt, and submits it. The company repays the exact amount. This is the cleanest route for flights, hotels, train tickets, taxis and client meals.

**Flat per diem.** The company pays a fixed daily amount for a trip instead of collecting receipts. This is common for meals and small costs while traveling. The tax result depends on the rules for the type of trip and on the amount paid. Part of a per diem can stay outside taxable income. Any amount above the allowed level is taxable.

**Mixed approach.** The company pays hotel and transport against receipts and adds a flat amount for meals. Many employers prefer this because it cuts paperwork but keeps the large costs fully documented.

The trip location matters too. Rules differ between travel within the employee's usual work area, travel elsewhere in Italy and travel abroad. Your payroll provider should classify each trip before the payment is processed.

## What counts as a valid receipt

In Italy, a receipt is not a casual photo of a card slip. Payroll needs a proper tax document. In practice that means an invoice or a fiscal receipt that shows the supplier, the date and the amount. A card statement alone usually does not meet the standard.

A few habits help:

- Ask the supplier for a proper invoice or fiscal receipt at the time of purchase.
- Submit claims soon after the trip, not months later.
- Keep the original or a clear digital copy, as your provider requires.
- Tie each claim to a trip, a date and a business reason.

If the supporting document is missing, do not assume payroll will accept the claim. Without it, the amount may have to be taxed as pay.

## How a claim moves through payroll

The flow is short, but each step matters.

1. The employee submits the claim with documents and a short note on the purpose.
2. Someone on your side approves the business need.
3. Payroll checks the documents and classifies each line as non-taxable or taxable.
4. The payment goes out with the monthly payslip. Non-taxable items appear as a separate line.
5. Records are kept in case of an audit.

Timing is the part founders miss. Expense claims should reach payroll before the monthly cut-off. A claim that arrives late waits for the next cycle. Employees notice this quickly, so set a clear deadline and repeat it.

## Where things go wrong

**Paying through a US system.** A reimbursement sent from a US expense tool never touches Italian payroll. The employee may receive it as untracked income. That creates a compliance gap for both sides.

**Treating a stipend as a reimbursement.** A fixed monthly amount for home office or phone costs, paid with no receipts, is usually taxable pay. Calling it a reimbursement does not change that.

**Mixing personal and business costs.** Add-ons such as a personal meal on a business trip need to be separated before the claim is submitted.

**No written policy.** Without a policy, every claim becomes a debate. Write down what is covered, what proof is needed, and which per diem approach you use.

## A simple policy to start with

Keep it short. State which costs are covered. State that receipts must be proper Italian tax documents. State whether meals use a flat per diem or actual cost. Set a monthly submission deadline. Name who approves claims. Share it with every new hire in Italy on day one.

## The practical takeaway

Reimbursements in Italy are not hard, but they are not informal. Proof, classification and timing decide the tax result. Run every claim through Italian payroll, keep your policy clear, and confirm the treatment of each trip type with your payroll provider before you pay.
