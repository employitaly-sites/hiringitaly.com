# How Long It Takes to Hire in Italy With an Employer of Record

> What happens between a signed offer and the first payroll when you hire in Italy through an Employer of Record, and what speeds up or slows down the timeline.



You found your candidate. The offer is signed. Now the question every founder and HR lead asks: how long until this person is on payroll and working legally in Italy?

The honest answer is that it depends on a few inputs you control and a few you do not. This article walks through the timeline from signed offer to first payroll when you use an Employer of Record (EOR). It shows where time goes and what you can do to shorten it.

We do not quote fixed day counts here. Italian onboarding depends on the role, the contract type, the collective agreement that applies and the worker's own situation. Anyone who promises a single number for every case is guessing.

## What an EOR changes about the timeline

Without an EOR, you would set up a local entity before you could hire. That means registrations, a local bank account, social security and tax accounts, and a payroll provider. Each step has its own queue.

With an EOR, the legal employer already exists. It already has the registrations and the payroll process. Your hire becomes an employee of that entity, and you direct the daily work. The setup phase mostly disappears. What remains is the work specific to your hire.

## Stage 1: Intake and contract drafting

The clock starts when you send the signed offer and the hire's details. The EOR needs the basics: role, start date, salary, working hours, work location and the employee's personal data.

The EOR then maps the role to the right national collective agreement. This matters in Italy. The agreement sets minimum pay levels, notice periods, extra monthly payments and leave rules. It shapes the employment contract.

This stage is fast when your information is complete. It slows down when the job description is vague, the salary is not final, or the role could fit more than one collective agreement. Send a clear job description and a firm compensation figure on day one.

## Stage 2: Contract review and signature

The EOR sends the employment contract to the employee. Italian contracts are written in Italian, and the employee should be able to read and understand what they sign. Good providers supply an English version for your records.

Time here depends on the employee. If they are currently employed, they may need time to resign and serve notice. That notice period often sets your real start date. Italian notice periods can be long for senior roles, and they follow the rules of the employee's current agreement. Ask the candidate early what notice they owe.

## Stage 3: Right to work and documents

For an EU citizen, this stage is simple. The EOR collects identity documents, tax code and bank details.

For a non-EU citizen who is not yet in Italy, this is the stage that decides your timeline. Work authorization and visa steps involve government offices and consulates. The EOR can prepare and submit the files, but it cannot control how long the offices take. If your hire already lives in Italy with a valid permit to work, the process is much shorter.

If you plan to hire someone who needs a visa, tell your EOR before the offer is signed. Better still, ask about this during candidate selection.

## Stage 4: Mandatory registrations

Before the start date, the EOR must notify the relevant authorities of the new employment. It also registers the employee for social security and insurance. This is routine for an EOR and happens inside its existing accounts.

The key rule is simple. These notifications must be in place on time for the start date. That is why a late start-date change or late documents can push the start back. Plan the start date after the paperwork is complete, not before.

## Stage 5: First payroll

Payroll in Italy runs on a monthly cycle. Your first payroll depends on when the employee starts relative to the payroll cut-off. A hire who starts just after a cut-off may wait for the next cycle. A hire who starts well before it will usually be paid in the same cycle, with a partial period.

Ask your EOR for the cut-off date and the pay date. Then choose a start date with that calendar in mind.

## What speeds things up

- A complete, accurate intake form on the first day.
- A clear job description that maps to one collective agreement.
- A candidate who is already in Italy with the right to work.
- Early questions about notice periods.
- A start date chosen around the payroll calendar.

## What slows things down

- Visa or work permit needs.
- Long notice periods at the candidate's current employer.
- Salary changes after contract drafting begins.
- Missing personal documents.
- Last-minute changes to the start date.

## The practical view

Think of the timeline as a chain. The longest link sets the pace. For most EU-based hires, that link is the candidate's notice period. For non-EU hires, it is usually the work authorization process. The EOR's own steps are the shortest part, because the legal employer and payroll are already in place.

To plan well, ask your EOR for a written timeline for your specific hire before you sign the offer. A good provider will show you each stage and tell you which ones depend on third parties.
