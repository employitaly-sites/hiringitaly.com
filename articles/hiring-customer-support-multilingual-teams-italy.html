Italy is a practical base for EU customer support. You can find agents who speak Italian natively and often add English, French, Spanish or German. This guide covers how to plan language coverage and set up the team.

Start with the customers, not the job post  

Before you write a job description, list where your tickets come from. Look at your current volume by language. Look at which markets are growing. Look at which channels customers use: email, chat, phone or social.

Then decide what each agent must do. A support agent who handles Italian tickets all day needs native-level Italian. An agent who covers overflow in several languages needs strong reading and writing in each one.

Define language levels clearly  

Vague requirements lead to bad hires. “Fluent English” means different things to different people. Use a shared scale, such as the CEFR levels, and write the level you need for speaking, writing and reading.

Some roles need more than others. Phone support needs clear speech and quick listening. Written support needs accurate grammar and tone. Test for the skill the job actually uses.

Good tests are simple:

  • A short writing task: reply to a real, anonymized ticket.
  • A live call or chat role-play in each required language.
  • A comprehension check on a product article.

Score every candidate the same way. Keep notes. This also helps if you need to explain a hiring decision later.

Choose a team model  

There are three common setups.

Language-dedicated agents. Each agent covers one market. Quality is high and training is simple. Coverage gets thin when someone is out.

Multilingual generalists. Each agent handles several languages. Coverage is flexible. These people are harder to find and often cost more.

A mix. A core of native speakers for your biggest markets, plus a few generalists for overflow and smaller languages. Most growing teams end up here.

Start small. Hire your first agents for the language with the most volume. Add languages as the data supports them.

Plan coverage across time zones  

Your customers in the US and your customers in Europe do not wake up at the same time. Decide which hours your Italian team covers. A team in Italy can handle European business hours well. It can also take the early part of the US day.

If you need round-the-clock support, plan handoffs. Write clear notes on open tickets. Use one shared queue and one shared knowledge base. Do not let each region build its own process.

Working hours, overtime and rest periods are set by Italian rules and the applicable collective agreement. Check these before you promise shift patterns in an offer. Your Employer of Record can confirm what is allowed for your role.

Pick the right contract and collective agreement  

In Italy, a national collective agreement (CCNL) usually applies to each role. It sets pay floors, hours, leave and notice terms. Customer support roles often fall under a commerce, services or telecommunications agreement, depending on what the company does.

The choice matters. It changes your cost and your obligations. Do not guess. Confirm the correct agreement before you publish a salary range.

Use an Employer of Record to move fast  

Most US companies do not have an Italian entity when they start hiring support staff. An Employer of Record (EOR) employs the agents on your behalf. You direct their daily work. The EOR handles the contract, payroll, social contributions and compliance.

This lets you test the market before you commit to a local company. You can hire your first agents, learn what works, and decide later whether to set up your own entity.

Onboard for quality from day one  

Support quality depends on training. Give new agents:

  • A written style guide for each language.
  • Access to the same tools and knowledge base as your US team.
  • A buddy who knows the product.
  • A clear escalation path to engineering and billing.

Translate your macros and help articles with care. Do not rely on raw machine translation for customer-facing text. Let native agents review and improve them. They know what sounds natural.

Keep the team connected  

Remote and distributed teams drift apart. Hold regular team meetings. Share customer feedback with product. Let Italian agents flag issues that only show up in their market, such as local payment methods or invoice rules.

Track quality by language. Use the same measures for each team: response time, resolution rate and customer satisfaction. Compare them honestly.

A simple plan  

  1. List your ticket volume by language and channel.
  2. Define language levels and build a short test.
  3. Pick a team model and start with your largest market.
  4. Confirm the collective agreement and working hours.
  5. Hire through an EOR and onboard with a clear playbook.
  6. Review quality by language, then add the next one.

A small, well-trained team beats a large, rushed one. Get the language mix right and the rest follows.
