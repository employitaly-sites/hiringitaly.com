How to Explain Net Pay to an Italian Employee

A plain guide for managers on walking an Italian employee through gross-to-net: pension contributions, income tax brackets and the employment tax credit.

Your new hire in Italy looks at the payslip and asks why the net is so far from the gross. You need a clear answer. This guide gives you one.

The short version: the gross salary goes through two main steps. First, the employee’s pension contribution comes out. Then income tax is calculated on what remains, and a tax credit reduces that tax.

Start with the order of the steps  

In Italy, gross-to-net follows a fixed sequence. Explain it in that order and the payslip stops looking random.

  1. Gross salary.
  2. Social security contribution paid by the employee.
  3. Taxable income (gross minus that contribution).
  4. Income tax (IRPEF) by bracket.
  5. Employment tax credit, which lowers the IRPEF.
  6. Net pay.

Write these on a slide or in a short email. Employees remember a list better than a paragraph.

Step one: the pension contribution  

The employee pays a share of pay into the national pension system through INPS. The pension (IVS) rate for employees is 9.19% of the taxable base for contributions.

Tell the employee what this is for. It builds their state pension and related cover. It is not a fee. It also reduces the income on which tax is calculated, which many people do not know.

A note on scope: other contributions and local items can appear on a payslip. This article covers the main mechanics. Your payroll provider can explain any extra line.

Step two: income tax by bracket  

IRPEF is progressive. That means different slices of income are taxed at different rates. It does not mean the whole salary is taxed at one rate.

The national brackets are:

  • Up to 28,000 euros a year: 23%.
  • From 28,000 to 50,000 euros: 33%.
  • Above 50,000 euros: 43%.

The most common mistake is thinking that crossing a threshold taxes the full salary at the higher rate. It does not. Only the part of income above the threshold is taxed at the higher rate. Say this out loud in the conversation. Employees who earn near a threshold often worry about a raise for exactly this reason.

Step three: the employment tax credit  

After IRPEF is calculated, employees get a tax credit for employment income. It is subtracted from the tax, not from the salary. This is why the effective tax rate is lower than the bracket rate.

The credit depends on income:

  • Up to 15,000 euros: 1,955 euros, with a minimum of 690 euros (1,380 euros for fixed-term contracts).
  • From 15,000 to 28,000 euros: 1,910 euros plus 1,190 euros scaled by how far income is below 28,000, over a range of 13,000.
  • From 28,000 to 50,000 euros: 1,910 euros scaled by how far income is below 50,000, over a range of 22,000.
  • An extra 65 euros applies for income between 25,000 and 35,000 euros.
  • Above 50,000 euros: no credit.

Two points matter for the conversation. The credit shrinks as income rises. And above 50,000 euros it is gone. A manager does not need to recite the formula. You need to say that the credit exists, that it falls with income, and that payroll applies it automatically.

How to run the conversation  

Use the employee’s own payslip. Generic examples feel abstract. Go through the real lines one by one.

Separate gross from cost. The employee sees gross and net. The company sees total cost. Do not mix the two. If the employee asks what the company pays, answer that separately.

Do not promise a net figure from memory. The net depends on the contract type, the income level and the payroll calculation. Give the method, then let payroll produce the number.

Explain what changes with a raise. A raise moves part of income into a higher bracket only for the portion above the threshold. At the same time, the tax credit may fall. Both effects are normal. Tell the employee before the raise lands, not after.

Offer a follow-up. Some people need time with the numbers. Say they can come back with questions after they have read the payslip at home.

What to avoid  

  • Do not say “the state takes a percentage” as if it were one flat rate. There are several steps.
  • Do not quote a net salary you have not checked.
  • Do not treat the pension contribution as a tax. The employee gets something for it.
  • Do not skip the tax credit. Without it, the explanation does not add up.

A script you can use  

“Your gross salary is the starting point. First, your pension contribution comes out. Then we calculate income tax on the rest, by bracket. Each bracket only applies to the slice of income inside it. Finally, a tax credit for employees reduces the tax. What is left is your net pay.”

That is enough for most first conversations. Go deeper only if the employee asks.
