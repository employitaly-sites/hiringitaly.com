# Equity and Stock Options for Italian Employees: A Primer

> How US equity plans meet Italian employment: tax, payroll, contracts and filings. A high-level primer for founders, HR, finance and legal ops.



You want your Italian hires to own a piece of the company. That is a fair goal. It is also where many US teams hit surprises. A plan that works well in the US does not carry over unchanged. This primer covers the main points to check before you grant anything.

## The core issue: the plan is yours, the employment is Italian

A US equity plan is written under US corporate and securities rules. An Italian employee works under Italian employment, tax and social security rules. Both sets of rules apply at the same time. Neither one switches the other off.

So the question is never just "can we grant options in Italy?" Usually you can. The real questions are how the grant is taxed, who withholds, and how it fits the employment contract.

## Equity is usually treated as pay

In the US, many teams think of equity as an investment upside. In Italy, equity given because of a job is generally treated as compensation in kind. That has consequences.

- Income tax can arise on the benefit the employee receives.
- Social security contributions can apply to that benefit as well.
- The employer, not the employee, is often responsible for withholding and reporting.

The exact treatment depends on the type of award, the moment of the taxable event, and the terms of the plan. Stock options, restricted stock units and direct share purchases can each be handled differently. Do not assume they follow the same path.

## The cash problem

This is the most common practical issue. An employee may owe tax when they receive shares or exercise options. But the shares may be illiquid, especially in a private company. The employee has a tax bill and no cash to pay it.

In the US, companies often solve this with net settlement or sell-to-cover. These tools may or may not fit your plan, and they may need extra steps in Italy. Decide early how tax will be funded. Then write it into the plan documents.

## Who withholds: the local employer question

If you employ people in Italy through your own Italian entity, that entity runs payroll. It must withhold tax and contributions on the equity benefit and report it. That means your US team has to send grant, vesting and valuation data to Italian payroll on time. Late data creates late withholding.

If you use an Employer of Record (EOR), the EOR is the legal employer and runs payroll. The same data flow still applies. The EOR needs accurate information on each grant, each vesting event and each exercise. It also needs to know who pays the tax cost when it is due. Agree this in writing before the first grant, not after.

## Valuation matters

For private companies, the value of the shares drives the taxable amount. You need a defensible valuation process, and you need to share the result with Italian payroll. A weak or undocumented valuation can cause disputes with the tax authorities later.

## Employment law touches equity too

Equity terms sit next to the employment contract. A few points deserve attention:

- **Vesting and termination.** Italian employment law is protective of employees. Forfeiture clauses that look standard in the US may need review for an Italian worker.
- **Plan terms as pay.** If equity is seen as part of regular pay, it can affect other entitlements. Draft the plan so it is clear that awards are discretionary and separate from base pay, where that is the intent.
- **Language and clarity.** Give employees plan documents they can understand. Provide an Italian version where useful. Unclear terms are more likely to be read against the employer.
- **Data and consent.** Equity administration shares personal data with US providers. Handle that under EU data protection rules.

## Securities and filing questions

Offering shares to employees can also raise securities-law questions, in the US and locally. Many employee plans qualify for exemptions or simplified treatment, but this should be confirmed for your plan and your structure. Some arrangements also involve reporting by the employee, such as declaring foreign-held assets. Tell your people this early. Surprises here fall on them personally.

## Alternatives some companies use

If a standard option plan is hard to run, there are other routes. Companies sometimes use phantom equity or cash-settled awards that track share value. These can reduce securities and ownership complexity. They have their own tax and drafting issues, so they are not a free shortcut. The right choice depends on your stage, your cap table and how much administration you can handle.

## A practical checklist

1. Decide the award type before you promise anything in an offer letter.
2. Confirm the tax and social security treatment with local advisers.
3. Plan how the employee will fund tax at the taxable event.
4. Set up a data flow from your equity platform to Italian payroll.
5. Document the valuation method.
6. Review forfeiture, leaver and discretionary-award wording against Italian employment law.
7. Tell employees what they will need to report and when.

## Do not promise in the offer letter

A common mistake is to put a headline equity number in an offer letter before anyone has checked the Italian side. Candidates compare offers. If the real after-tax result is lower than they expected, trust suffers. Keep early messages accurate and general. Share details once the plan has been reviewed for Italy.

## Bottom line

Equity for Italian employees is workable. It needs planning on tax, payroll, valuation and contract language. Treat it as part of total compensation cost, not as a free add-on. Get local advice on your specific plan before the first grant.
